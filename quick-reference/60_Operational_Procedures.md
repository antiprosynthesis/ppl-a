# Operational Procedures — Complete Exam Cheat Sheet

**Scope:** 60 questions in `60_Operational_Procedures.md`; keyed answers take precedence where a simplified exam convention differs from a broader rule. For real operations, the current Belgian AIP, ATC instructions, aircraft POH/AFM and checklist prevail. A memory sheet never replaces aircraft-specific procedures.

## Rapid recognition map

| Exam clue | Recall |
|---|---|
| Village/noise | Avoid turns while climbing over villages; circumnavigate and cross high. Arrival: high, low power, delay descent/configuration, published route. |
| Sudden unexplained IAS/altitude/ROC/ROD change | Wind shear. Inversion and thunderstorms are clues. |
| Microburst | Headwind gain → IAS may rise; core downdraft → climb performance falls; outflow/tailwind → IAS falls. Avoid storm; if caught, max power, keep configuration, best climb-rate speed. |
| Heavy aircraft ahead | Wingtip vortices: strongest heavy, clean, slow; lift-off starts when its nosewheel leaves. Stay above its path and land beyond its touchdown point. |
| Smoke/fire | Identify source; use its POH checklist. Electrical/cable fire: master OFF first. Engine fire smoke entering cabin: heat/ventilation OFF. |
| Emergency vs precautionary | Emergency = circumstances force landing. Precautionary = landing chosen to preserve safety before forced. |
| Wet/contaminated runway | More rolling resistance/longer distances, poor braking and directional control; standing water → aquaplaning. |
| Flight over water out of gliding range | Accessible life-jacket or flotation device for each occupant; life vest inflated only after exiting. |
| Hot brakes/wheels | Tyre may burst axially; approach from front or rear, not from the side. |

## 1. Noise abatement — Q1–3, Q38

### Departures near villages
1. Keep the aircraft safely climbing; **avoid turns at low altitude over villages during climb**.
2. **Circumnavigate villages** and cross them only at sufficient altitude.
3. Follow published noise-abatement routes and any ATC clearance; never trade obstacle clearance or safety for noise reduction.

### Descent and approach
- Use a **high approach**, minimum practicable power, **late descent**, and **late configuration**; remain on established arrival routes.
- Avoid a low, powered approach, unnecessary early configuration, or a shortcut/direct route that conflicts with the published route.
- The noise perceived on the ground is affected by **engine power, propeller RPM, landing gear, flap setting, ground track and height above ground**. Flight rules do not themselves change noise emission.
- Reduce power/RPM only within safe engine limits and aircraft procedures. Avoid abrupt or unsafe manoeuvres.

**Mnemonic:** *High, quiet, late, on-route* (high approach, low power, late descent/configuration, published route).

## 2. Bird strike — not tested in this 60-question bank

Beyond-bank awareness only: prioritise aircraft control; avoid abrupt manoeuvres; assess engine, flight controls, windscreen and other damage; advise ATC and land at a suitable aerodrome if damage or abnormal operation is suspected. Follow the aircraft checklist. No bank question tests a bird-strike checklist or a numerical avoidance rule.

## 3. Wind shear and microbursts — Q11–21, Q42–44

### Definition, cues and conditions
- **Wind shear:** a vertical or horizontal change in wind speed **and/or** direction over a short distance. It is not defined by a particular 15-kt threshold or altitude.
- Likely with **temperature inversions**, thunderstorms, heavy showers and near the surface. Thunderstorms are a typical associated phenomenon.
- Recognise it by a sudden, apparently baseless change in **altitude, IAS, rate of climb or rate of descent**.
- Avoid take-off/landing during passage of heavy showers or thunderstorms; do not fly into a known microburst.

### Effect on approach/climb if the pilot makes no correction

| Wind change | IAS response | Flight-path response | Exam application |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Increasing headwind (or decreasing tailwind) | Increases | Path tends higher | Approach Q16/Q18 |
| Decreasing headwind (or increasing tailwind) | Decreases | Path tends lower | Approach Q19; after take-off Q17 |
| Microburst outflow transition to tailwind | Decreases | Sink/climb performance worsens | Figure point 3; Q14/Q44 |
| Microburst downdraft/core | May initially be unchanged | Rate of climb decreases / descent may increase | Figure point 2; Q15 |

Why: wind speed changes first alter airflow over the wing and IAS/lift; a transient headwind gain can lift the aircraft above the intended path, while a loss of headwind/tailwind gain lowers IAS and path. In a microburst the downdraft can overwhelm climb performance even if IAS initially rises.

### If encountered near the ground
- **Prevention is best:** avoid storm cells/heavy showers; delay take-off or landing.
- If inadvertently in a microburst after lift-off: **maximum power; maintain the present configuration; fly the recommended best-rate-of-climb speed** (as keyed in this bank). Do not retract gear/flaps, turn to escape, or accelerate away unless the aircraft's approved procedure specifically directs it.
- In a generic wind-shear encounter, **maintain configuration and adjust thrust**; do not make a large configuration change or chase airspeed with abrupt pitch inputs. Follow the POH/AFM and training.

### Read the bank's microburst figure (OPR-001)
1. Find the numbered aircraft and identify whether it is entering, beneath, or leaving the downdraft/outflow.
2. **Point 1 / entry:** headwind increases; IAS can rise; climb rate can vary.
3. **Point 2 / core:** downdraft reduces rate of climb.
4. **Point 3 / exit/outflow:** headwind is lost / tailwind develops; IAS decreases, with degraded climb performance.
5. Match the question to the variable it asks for; do not answer “airspeed” when it asks “climb rate.”

## 4. Wake turbulence and jet blast — Q22–25, Q45–49

### Cause and strongest conditions
- A lifting wing has higher pressure below and lower pressure above. Pressure equalisation curls around each wingtip and forms **counter-rotating wingtip vortices** behind the aircraft.
- Vortex strength increases with **aircraft weight**, and is greatest for a **heavy, clean (low lift-device deflection), slow** aircraft. For the same type, mass and configuration, the slower aircraft produces stronger wake.
- Vortices descend and drift with wind; they can remain hazardous after the preceding aircraft has passed.
- During take-off the vortices start when the preceding aircraft **lifts off its nosewheel** (bank wording). Do not wait for a particular height or for the main gear to leave.

### Practical avoidance
- **Take-off behind a larger aircraft:** if possible, rotate before its rotation point and climb above its flight path; avoid its path and touchdown/rotation area. Be alert for vortices left on the runway.
- A **slight crosswind** may leave a vortex on or near the runway; do not assume it has blown clear.
- **Landing behind a medium/heavy airliner on a long runway:** approach above the preceding aircraft's approach path, then touch down **beyond its nose-gear touchdown point** (bank's keyed wording). Do not land before its touchdown point or fly through its wake.
- **Taxi behind an airliner:** Q25 tests **jet blast**, not wake spacing: maintain at least **200 m** in the keyed answer. Follow marshaller/ATC and airport instructions; jet blast can move people/equipment and damage aircraft.

## 5. Emergency, precautionary and off-field landings — Q26–28, Q31, Q50–55, Q60

### Definitions: choose by the reason for landing
| Term | Meaning | Do not confuse with |
|---|---|---|
| **Emergency landing** | Circumstances force the aircraft to land. | Not necessarily an engine-off landing; “forced by circumstances” is the key. |
| **Precautionary landing** | Pilot elects to land to sustain/preserve flight safety. | Not yet a forced landing. |
| **Off-field / outlanding** | Landing at an unprepared site; describes the site, not whether the reason is emergency or precautionary. | Not automatically engine failure. |

### Emergency off-field sequence tested by Q31
1. **Locate a suitable landing area.**
2. **Plan the approach.**
3. Complete the applicable POH emergency procedure.
4. Declare the emergency when appropriate / as time permits.
5. Begin the approach in good time and **concentrate on flying it**.

Keep control and landing area selection ahead of administrative tasks; use the aircraft's actual checklist. The bank's keyed sequence includes area selection and approach planning before checklist and declaration.

### Site assessment and technique
- Inspect the site and surrounding terrain, wind, slope, surface, obstacles, approach/departure paths and go-around options; identify approach points before committing.
- Prefer a **harvested/short-crop field** over ploughed ground, long dry grass, ripe waving crops, or a sports area near people. A light-brown field with short crops is the bank's suitable visual cue.
- For a bumpy surface (glider question), touch down at **minimum safe speed** and keep the elevator pulled back until stopped (Q60). Do not add speed or push forward at first contact.
- A precautionary landing with hot brakes/wheels: approach from **front or rear**, not from either side. An overheated tyre may burst **axially**; keep clear of the wheel's side-on line of fire. Do not cool with water or remove fairings.

### Passenger emergency briefing — Q53
Brief, as time and situation allow: **nature of emergency; pilot's intentions; brace/safety position; how/when to evacuate and exits; what to do after landing**. Give concise, actionable directions; account for occupants who need assistance.

## 6. Ditching and flights over water — Q29–32

- If land cannot be reached following an engine failure, carry **life vests or a life raft for every occupant** (Q29–30). Keep equipment accessible and brief passengers.
- In a ditching, **inflate life jackets only after leaving the aircraft**; inflating inside can impede exit (Q32).
- Know exits, release/door operation, restraint/brace position and raft location before flight. Follow the aircraft checklist and survival-equipment instructions.
- Lesson-slide regulatory excerpt (EASA NCO.IDE.A.175) says life-jackets/individual flotation devices apply to specified operations including single-engine landplanes beyond gliding distance from land, operations where take-off/approach is disposed over water with likelihood of ditching, seaplanes over water, and operations at specified distances from land. Check the current rule and operation; the bank's safe answer is flotation for all occupants when land is unreachable.

**Lesson-only C150 checklist example (not a universal ditching procedure):** transmit on the indicated emergency frequency, secure heavy objects, consider wind/swell (into wind in heavy sea; parallel to swell in light wind/high swell), use the aircraft-specific flap/approach speeds, protect face, evacuate, then inflate the vest/raft after egress. Apply only if that exact aircraft POH says so.

## 7. Fire, smoke and carbon monoxide — Q4–10, Q39–41

### Bank-tested facts
- Carburettor fire risk applies to **piston engines**; pumping the throttle during a cold-weather start can over-prime the engine and cause a **carburettor fire**.
- An engine that has been on fire must **not be restarted**: reignition risk is too high.
- Smoke from the instrument panel / suspected electrical or cable fire: first action is **master switch OFF** (Q7–8), then follow the aircraft checklist.
- Engine fire with smoke entering cockpit: turn **heating and ventilation OFF** immediately (Q9). For suspected engine fire, the bank's keyed first action is **turn off the heat** (Q10).
- The most dangerous gas in the bank's engine-fire question is **carbon monoxide (CO)**. CO is toxic and can be present in exhaust smoke.
- Of the listed extinguishing agents, **water is least suitable** for aircraft fire (Q41); use an agent compatible with the fire and aircraft electrical/fuel hazards.

### Source-specific response logic
1. **Fly/control first.** Identify the likely source; use the matching POH/AFM checklist immediately.
2. **Electrical fire:** isolate electrical power as directed (bank's first step: master OFF); switch off nonessential electrical loads as checklist directs; shut cabin air/heat if smoke is entering; use extinguisher if needed; ventilate after discharge only when safe.
3. **Engine fire in flight:** apply the engine-fire checklist (fuel/mixture, heat/air, airspeed, landing/forced-landing actions are aircraft-specific); land as soon as possible. Don't use a single generic “master off” response for an engine fire.
4. **Carburettor fire during start:** use the start-fire checklist; do not pump throttle. If a fire occurred, do not restart an engine that was previously on fire.

The lesson includes C150/APCK examples: electrical-fire card starts with master OFF and non-ignition electrical switches OFF; cabin fire card closes cabin air/heat and uses the extinguisher; engine-fire/start cards include mixture/fuel-valve and landing actions. They are **examples only**; switch order and speeds vary by aircraft.

### Extinguisher matching (lesson-slide “A–F” chart; exact agent suitability varies)

| Fire class in slide | Material | Agents shown suitable |
|---|---|---|
| A | Ordinary solids (e.g. paper/wood) | Water, foam, powder, wet chemical |
| B | Flammable liquids | Foam, powder, CO₂ |
| C | Flammable gases | Powder |
| D | Flammable metals | Powder |
| E | Electrical equipment | Powder, CO₂ |
| F | Cooking fats/oils | Wet chemical |

Do not use the chart as a replacement for the extinguisher label or aircraft procedure. Water is particularly dangerous on live electrical equipment and burning fuel; the bank asks simply which is least suitable overall: **water**.

## 8. Runway contamination, wet surfaces and aquaplaning — Q33–36, Q56–59

### Contaminated-runway definition and threshold
- Bank's full definition (Q33): the runway is contaminated when **more than 25%** of the runway surface area within the required length and width being used is covered by **water, slush, snow or ice more than 3 mm deep**.
- Q56 abbreviates the threshold to **25%**; remember the exam threshold as 25% (strict wording in Q33: *more than* 25%). Do not choose 50% or 75%.
- The bank's Q33 keyed option is the one specifying >25%, the required runway area, the listed contaminants and >3 mm depth. Frost/sand are distractor wording in that item.

### Surface effects
| Surface / condition | Exam effect |
|---|---|
| Wet grass | Take-off **and** landing distances increase. |
| Wet snow | Increased rolling resistance on take-off. |
| Standing water | Increased rolling resistance on take-off; may also cause spray and hydroplaning. |
| Water after heavy rain | Tyre may ride on water, reducing braking; landing roll/braking distance increases. |
| Snow/ice/slush/contaminants | Reduced friction, braking and directional control; performance can differ greatly from dry-runway data. |

### Aquaplaning / hydroplaning response
- **Recognise:** poor deceleration or directional control on a water-covered surface; tyre/runway contact and braking effectiveness are reduced. The result is a **longer braking distance**, not shorter.
- If suspected on landing and all wheels are rolling: **brake moderately** and maintain directional control with aerodynamic means (Q58). Avoid maximum braking; do not cross-control to “airbrake” with the fuselage.
- If contaminated-runway landing is unavoidable (Q59): use the **minimum crosswind component**, **maximum flaps**, touchdown **firmly** at minimum safe speed, and brake carefully. Do not float/land softly, choose minimum flaps, or assume braking is unnecessary.
- Use approved runway-condition/performance data; no single dry-runway stopping distance applies.

### Exam trap
“Contaminated” is a defined coverage/depth condition, not simply “any wet runway.” But even a runway below the formal definition can still be slippery and operationally hazardous.

## 9. Flight time — Q37

For this bank, **flight time** is the total time from the aircraft's first movement until it finally comes to rest at the end of the flight. Choose the option that says first movement → final rest, not take-off run → touchdown or engine start → shutdown. For precise legal logging, use the current rule's aircraft-specific definition and operator procedure.

## 11. Figure-reading and exam method

1. Classify the topic from the stem: weather change, vortex, fire source, landing reason, surface condition, or over-water exposure.
2. Underline the requested result: *IAS, flight path, climb rate, first action, definition, distance, or equipment*.
3. For a diagram, identify aircraft/point, airflow arrows, preceding/succeeding category, units and direction before choosing.
4. For multiple-choice wording, distinguish “emergency” from “precautionary,” “wake” from “jet blast,” and “approach path” from IAS.
5. Where a question asks “first,” select the immediate keyed action; where it asks for a full response, use the aircraft-specific checklist.
6. If an option changes gear/flaps or calls for a turn in a microburst, it is not the keyed action: preserve the present configuration and maximise climb performance.
