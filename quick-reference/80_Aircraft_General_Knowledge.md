# Aircraft General Knowledge — Complete Exam Cheat Sheet

A bank-focused reference for all 128 questions in `80_Aircraft_General_Knowledge.md`. Use the aircraft POH/AFM, checklists, placards and approved maintenance data for real operations: systems, speeds, limits and procedures are type-specific. Where a bank key conflicts with the lesson shorthand, the exam-key answer is called out below.

## 1. Airframe, structure and loads

### Main components and wing construction

| Part / term | What to identify or remember |
|---|---|
| Fuselage | Carries occupants, payload and systems; transfers loads between components. |
| Wing | Produces lift; leading edge is forward, trailing edge aft, root joins fuselage, tip is outboard. |
| Empennage (tail) | Horizontal tail (stabilizer/elevator) and vertical tail (fin/rudder). A T-tail has the horizontal tail mounted atop the fin. |
| Spar | Main spanwise structural beam; carries major bending loads. |
| Ribs | Crosswise members that give the wing its aerofoil/profile shape and transfer loads to spars. |
| Stringers | Longitudinal members supporting skin and stiffening fuselage/wing panels. |
| Frames/formers | Cross-sectional fuselage members that define its shape and distribute loads. |
| Skin/planking | Outer covering; may be non-structural or stressed/load-carrying depending on construction. |
| Wing thickness | Vertical distance between upper and lower surfaces at the **thickest** part of the aerofoil. |

### Structural types

| Type | Load-carrying construction | Key recognition |
|---|---|---|
| Grid / truss / space-frame | Tubular framework (often welded steel) carries loads; fabric or thin skin is not self-supporting. | “Framework with covering”; a tube lattice is the structure. |
| Monocoque | The shell/skin carries most of the load, with little internal framework. | “Mono = shell”; skin is structural. |
| Semi-monocoque | Frames + stringers + stressed skin share loads. | Typical metal fuselage; “semi” does not mean skin is merely a cover. |
| Sandwich / honeycomb | Two thin face skins bonded to a light core (honeycomb or foam). | High stiffness, stability and strength for low mass; faces carry bending, core supports/separates them. |

**Materials:** for the bank’s comparison, carbon-fibre reinforced plastic is the highest-strength choice. This is a question-bank comparison, not a universal rule that every composite part is stronger than every metal part. Wood, aluminium, magnesium and composites have different strength, stiffness, density, fatigue, corrosion, repair and temperature properties.

### Wing and tail configurations / figure reading

- **High wing:** wing attached high on fuselage. **Strut-braced high wing:** visible diagonal struts connect wing to fuselage (bank figure `AGK-002`). Do not confuse “high wing” with the more specific “strut-braced high wing.”
- **T-tail:** horizontal stabilizer sits on top of vertical fin (`AGK-003`). A V-tail has two diagonal surfaces; cruciform tail mounts the tail partway up the fin.
- Read structural drawings by following labels: spar runs spanwise; ribs run chordwise; stringers run lengthwise; skin encloses the framework.
- Propeller diagram `AGK-011`: blade **angle of incidence/pitch** is the angle between blade chord and plane of rotation; blade angle of attack is between chord and the local relative airflow. Identify the airflow arrow before naming the angle.

### Aircraft load and inspection

- Load factor: **n = lift / weight**, conventionally expressed in g. In a level coordinated turn, lift must exceed weight; greater bank increases load factor and stall speed.
- Gusts and abrupt manoeuvres increase structural loads. The bank tests damage risk from exceeding manoeuvring speed in heavy gusts; do not treat manoeuvring speed as permission to make abrupt/full control inputs in every condition.
- Dents, creases or deformation can indicate hard landing or excessive stress. They are structural clues, not merely cosmetic faults; report and have damage assessed.
- **Exam-key maintenance item:** after permissible limitations have been exceeded, the bank answer is that a qualified engineer must inspect the aircraft before its next flight. The question wording oddly says “helicopter”; learn the bank key, but do not use this as a substitute for the applicable aircraft’s approved inspection requirements.

## 2. Axes, primary and secondary controls

### Three axes and controls

| Axis (body axis) | Motion | Primary control | Hand/pedal cue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Longitudinal (nose to tail) | Roll | Ailerons | Move control wheel/stick left/right. |
| Lateral (wingtip to wingtip) | Pitch | Elevator (or stabilator) | Push/pull. |
| Vertical (top to bottom) | Yaw | Rudder | Press pedals. |

**Mnemonic:** A-E-R = **A**ileron-roll, **E**levator-pitch, **R**udder-yaw. Axis names describe the axis of rotation, not the direction the control surface moves.

- Aft stick/yoke → elevator trailing edge up → tail downforce increases (typical conventional-tail aeroplane) → nose pitches up. Forward input reverses this. Aircraft configuration can differ; use the aircraft’s control description.
- Left rudder → yaw left; the secondary effect in the bank is roll left. Rudder-induced sideslip/yaw couples into roll.
- Small aircraft controls are normally moved **manually by rods and/or cables**, not powered fly-by-wire or hydraulic actuators.
- **Bank helicopter item:** in cruise, pushing the stick forward without other corrections increases forward speed and increases sink rate. This is a helicopter-specific keyed result, not a fixed-wing control rule.
- Primary flight controls are **aileron, elevator, rudder**. Secondary controls include **flaps, leading-edge devices (slats), spoilers/speedbrakes and trim systems**. Secondary controls improve performance/handling or relieve sustained control force; they are not backups for the primary controls.

### High-lift, drag and trim devices

| Device | Main job / exam cue |
|---|---|
| Flap | Trailing-edge high-lift device; increases lift and drag, enabling slower approach/landing. White ASI arc is flap operating range. |
| Slat / leading-edge device | Helps maintain attached flow at higher angle of attack; increases usable lift / delays stall. |
| Spoiler / speedbrake | Spoils lift and increases drag; may assist roll or descent depending on design. |
| Winglet | Weakens wingtip vortex and reduces induced drag (bank answer). |
| Trim | Relieves control force / balances the aircraft at a chosen condition; it does **not** move the centre of gravity. |
| Elevator trim tab | For the bank’s conventional arrangement: nose-up trim → tab down and elevator up. Moving trim wheel aft gives tab down/elevator up. Verify type-specific rigging. |
| Balance tab | Moves opposite to its associated control surface to reduce hinge/control force. |
| Ground-adjustable tab | Fixed/non-movable in flight; bent/adjusted on the ground to correct a persistent rigging or trim tendency. |
| Anti-servo tab | **Beyond bank:** on some stabilators it moves with the trailing edge and increases feel/stability; not the same as a balance tab. |

A quick way to solve tab questions: distinguish **pilot trim command** from **tab direction** and **main control direction**. Follow the stated nose-up/nose-down effect, not just the word “aft.”

## 3. Landing gear, steering, wheels and brakes

- A nosewheel or tailwheel on a small aeroplane is commonly steered through the **rudder pedals**, linked to the rudder; pedal input can therefore steer both rudder and wheel on the ground.
- Bank gear diagram `AGK-005`: part **1 = fork** (wheel fork). Other pictured parts can include strut/shock absorber, torque links, axle and shimmy damper; first locate the number’s pointer, then trace it to the component.
- Main gear normally carries the wheel brakes in the bank question; **brakes are on the main wheels**, not only the nosewheel.
- Hydraulic toe-brake system: pedal/master cylinder → hydraulic line → brake unit at each main wheel. Parking brake traps/holds pressure in many designs.
- Tire inspection: tread, sidewall, cuts/wear, correct pressure per aircraft data, and tire-to-rim alignment marks. Bank figure `AGK-006` asks whether the mark shows correct tire position relative to rim; it is **not** a pressure indicator.
- If a tire has moved relative to the rim, report it; approved maintenance staff assess it. Tire markings also identify size, ply/load and speed ratings.

## 4. Piston engines and four-stroke cycle

### Common layouts and engine labels

- Small aeroplanes and touring motor gliders commonly use a **four-cylinder, four-stroke, horizontally opposed (boxer) piston engine**.
- Other layouts shown in class: radial/star, V, and in-line/inverted. The bank’s Q105 diagram is the horizontally opposed layout: cylinders lie opposite each other across the crankcase.
- Typical piston-engine parts: cylinder/head, piston and rings, connecting rod, crankshaft/crankcase, inlet/exhaust valves, camshaft, spark plug.
- Engine designation example from class, `LGTSIO-540-K`: the slide associates **G** geared, **T** turbocharged, **S** supercharged, **I** fuel-injected, **O** opposed, **L** left-turning; 540 is displacement in cubic inches and K is model series. Manufacturer conventions vary.
- **Beyond bank conversion:** 1 in³ = 16.387 cm³; 540 in³ ≈ 8,849 cm³ ≈ 8.85 L.

### Otto cycle: identify a drawing by piston/valve motion

| Stroke | Piston | Inlet valve | Exhaust valve | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Induction/intake | Moves down | Open | Closed | Air-fuel mixture enters. `AGK-007` = induction. |
| 2. Compression | Moves up | Closed | Closed | Mixture is compressed. `AGK-008` = compression. |
| 3. Power | Moves down | Closed | Closed | Spark ignites mixture; expanding gases drive piston down. |
| 4. Exhaust | Moves up | Closed | Open | Burned gases leave. |

A full four-stroke cycle takes **two crankshaft revolutions (720°)**; each cylinder has one power stroke per cycle. At the transition from exhaust to intake, both valves can be briefly open (**valve overlap**); this does not change the four stroke names.

### Power, mixture and engine protection

- Naturally aspirated carburettor-engine power normally **decreases with altitude** because air density/mass flow falls. A fixed mixture setting then becomes **richer** (less air for the fuel being metered).
- “Rich” = relatively more fuel; “lean” = relatively more air. Carburettor’s major job is to make an ignitable air/fuel mixture; throttle chiefly controls airflow/power, mixture control sets fuel/air ratio.
- Rich mixture clues from the lesson: higher fuel consumption, roughness, fouled/sooty plugs, black smoke/lead deposits and reduced power. It usually gives better cooling and delays detonation compared with an excessively lean setting.
- Excessively lean/high-temperature operation: high temperatures, longer/abnormal combustion, detonation risk and power loss. “Lean” is not always wrong: mixture is normally leaned for appropriate cruise conditions according to POH and engine instruments.
- **Detonation / knocking:** uncontrolled rapid end-gas combustion after the normal spark; risk is raised by excessive temperature, excessively lean mixture, unsuitable low-octane fuel, high manifold pressure with low RPM on a constant-speed propeller, or abruptly applying power. Prevent with correct fuel, cooling, mixture and power/RPM procedures.
- **Pre-ignition** is ignition before the intended spark (e.g. hot spot); it differs from detonation, which is abnormal combustion after normal ignition. Both can cause serious overheating/damage.
- Use the POH for leaning thresholds and power settings. The lesson gives operating examples around <75% power / altitude and says enrich before increasing power, enrich for descent/go-around, and use full rich for take-off/low-altitude conditions when the POH requires it. These are teaching cues, not universal settings; high/hot/density-altitude take-off may require leaning per POH.

## 5. Carburettor, induction, fuel and ignition

### Carburettor and carburettor heat

- A float carburettor uses a float/needle valve to maintain fuel level; the venturi speeds airflow and lowers pressure so metered fuel is drawn in and atomised. The throttle valve controls air passage; mixture control adjusts fuel/air ratio. The **primer** is an auxiliary fuel pump/primer arrangement that supplies fuel to help starting.
- **Carburettor icing:** fuel evaporation and pressure drop at the venturi cool the carburettor; ice can form even when outside air is above freezing. Bank’s best-likelihood answer is **−5°C to +20°C**. Lesson slide’s operational cue is **below +5°C in visible moisture**. Treat these as overlapping cues, not hard boundaries: icing is possible in a broad range of cool, moist conditions, and in some conditions without visible moisture.
- Carb heat supplies warm induction air and helps melt/prevent carb ice. Warm air is less dense and the mixture can become richer; on a fixed-pitch propeller, selecting carb heat normally causes an **RPM decrease** (bank answer). A temporary further drop can occur while ice melts, followed by recovery if icing was present.
- Switch carb heat **off for take-off** (bank answer) to preserve maximum power; use it in flight/checks as directed by POH/checklist. Do not ignore suspected icing during descent, low power, slow flight or approach.

### Fuel tanks, grade and refuelling

- Tank **vent** admits air as fuel is consumed, preventing a vacuum/underpressure that could stop fuel flow.
- Water is denser than avgas and collects at the **lowest point/sump**; drain fuel samples and check for water/contamination as required.
- A nearly empty tank has more air space and is more exposed to condensation; the bank says greatest humidity absorption is with **almost empty tanks**. Keep tanks appropriately full when parked if the aircraft/operation requires it.
- Avgas **100LL is blue** in the bank. Octane/fuel grade describes **anti-knock resistance**, not ignition timing, flame speed or combustion temperature. Always use the approved grade/type.
- Refuelling: no smoking/open flames, obey restrictions, and bond/ground the aircraft and fuelling equipment to reduce static-spark risk. Do not energise ignition/electrical systems while refuelling. Follow local and operator procedures.
- Static-discharge wicks/dischargers release accumulated static charge during flight to reduce radio interference; they are not substitutes for refuelling bonding.
- Fuel-quantity indication: the bank’s CS-23 statement is one indicator per tank, calibrated to read **zero in level flight when only unusable fuel remains**. Do not interpret “zero” as absolutely dry tank.

### Ignition and magneto checks

- Two independent magnetos (dual ignition) normally supply separate spark plugs; each magneto is engine-driven and works independently of the aircraft electrical system. Thus an electrical-system failure does not by itself stop magneto ignition.
- Magneto switch grounds the magneto primary circuit via the P-lead to stop ignition. On a properly grounded switch, briefly selecting OFF during the specified check causes RPM to fall, then selecting BOTH restores the original RPM. Never leave the switch OFF longer than the checklist directs; propeller may still be live if grounding is faulty.
- A defective/fouled spark plug is the likely cause of excessive roughness during a magneto check. Small RPM drop on one magneto is expected; use the aircraft’s stated maximum drop and difference.
- Engine shutdown in the bank: move mixture fully to idle cut-off (lean completely). Do not rely only on closing throttle or shutting off generator.

### Fuel injection (lesson extension)

- Fuel is metered/injected closer to the cylinders rather than evaporating in a carburettor venturi; this improves distribution and can improve metering/acceleration. It prevents **carburettor venturi ice**, but does not make the entire induction system immune to icing.
- The lesson notes vapour lock and contamination as potential problems. Follow the aircraft’s hot-start, priming and fuel-pressure procedures.

## 6. Oil, cooling, engine instruments and propeller

### Lubrication and cooling

- Oil functions: lubricates/reduces friction and wear, removes heat, carries fine abrasion/contaminants to the filter, helps clean/seal and damp noise. Oil has viscosity, flash-point and chemical-stability properties.
- If an oil filter clogs, the **bypass valve opens** so oil circulation can continue, but oil is **not filtered** in bypass. It is not safe to assume an alternate filter catches the debris.
- Air-cooled engines use cylinder cooling fins to enlarge surface area and transfer heat quickly to airflow; cowling/baffles guide air over hot areas. Oil also carries heat to an oil cooler. Cowl flaps (if fitted) regulate cooling airflow.
- Too little cooling can cause high CHT/oil temperature, detonation, seizure/damage and shorter engine life; excessive cooling may also be harmful. Use POH power, mixture and cowl-flap guidance.
- Lesson maintenance reminders: check oil quantity before flight, observe pressure soon after start (slide says within ~30 s), monitor pressure and temperature in flight, look for leaks, and check/maintain oil on level ground with the aircraft/engine condition specified by the POH. The slide’s “yearly / every 50 hr” and “+4 / −2” shorthand is not a universal maintenance interval or limit; use approved aircraft documentation.

### Engine indications

| Indication | What it measures / recognition |
|---|---|
| Oil pressure | Pressure in lubrication system; a pressure instrument. |
| Oil temperature | Oil temperature; a temperature instrument. |
| CHT | Cylinder-head temperature; bank says indication relates to the **critical cylinder**, not a random cylinder or an average of all cylinders. |
| EGT | Exhaust-gas temperature; mixture/combustion trend indication (use POH). |
| Fuel pressure | Fuel-system pressure. |
| Manifold pressure (MAP) | Intake-manifold absolute pressure; pressure instrument; alongside RPM helps set power on a constant-speed propeller. |
| Suction/vacuum | Vacuum-system pressure/differential; indicates gyro vacuum supply when fitted. |
| Tachometer | Engine/propeller RPM, not pressure. |
| Fuel quantity | Tank level/quantity, not pressure. |
| OAT/cabin temperature | Ambient/cabin temperature. |

For the bank’s “which gauges measure temperature?” set: **oil, engine coolant (if fitted), CHT, EGT, outside air and cabin air**. A directional gyro, altimeter and VSI are not temperature gauges.

### Fixed-pitch and constant-speed propellers

- Fixed-pitch propeller: blade pitch is fixed; RPM varies with airspeed and engine power. Thus carb heat selection is seen as an RPM drop on a fixed-pitch engine.
- Constant-speed propeller: governor changes blade pitch to maintain selected RPM; pilot controls propeller RPM and throttle/MAP separately. MAP is manifold pressure; RPM is revolutions per minute.
- Unless the flight manual specifies otherwise, bank sequence for **increasing power**: **RPM up first, then MAP/throttle up**. For **reducing power**: **MAP/throttle down first, then RPM down**. This avoids high MAP with low RPM (a detonation-risk combination).
- When increasing power, mixture may also need to be enriched first (lesson cue); follow the aircraft’s checklist and POH.

## 7. Electrical system and hydraulics

### Electrical basics

| Quantity / component | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Voltage | Electrical potential; volts (V). |
| Current | Flow of charge; amperes (A). |
| Resistance | Opposition to current; ohms (Ω). |
| Electrical power | Rate of energy use; watts (W). Simplified DC relation: P = V × I. |
| Battery | Stores electrical energy and supplies power when alternator output is unavailable/insufficient. |
| Alternator/generator | Supplies electrical power with engine running and recharges battery. |
| Voltage regulator | Controls system voltage. |
| Bus bars | Distribute power to circuits. |
| Relays/solenoids | Electrically operated switches. |
| Circuit breakers/fuses | Protect wiring/circuits; they are not primary power sources. |

- Aircraft supply is principally **battery + generator/alternator**; relay and circuit breaker are not sources.
- On alternator failure, battery continues supplying loads for a limited time/remaining capacity; not every instrument immediately fails. Bank helicopter wording: **no change as long as battery has enough power**.
- Electrical failure can affect radio, navigation equipment and **electrically driven gyros/instruments**. Magnetic compass, pitot-static airspeed/altimeter/VSI and magneto ignition are not inherently powered by the electrical bus (some installations add electric backup/heating).
- Instrument marking **DC** means direct current.
- Hydraulic oil answer in this bank: **synthetic oil**. Actual fluid is system-specific; use only the fluid specified by the aircraft manufacturer.

## 8. Pitot-static system and airspeed

### Pressure principles and instruments

- **Static pressure** = ambient pressure of undisturbed airflow. **Pitot pressure** = total/stagnation pressure. Dynamic pressure is the difference: **q = total − static = ½ρV²**.
- Pitot head senses total pressure; static port senses ambient static pressure.
- **ASI** uses both pitot (total) and static pressure; the diaphragm sees their difference (dynamic pressure).
- **Altimeter** and **VSI** use static pressure only. Direct-reading magnetic compass does not use either pressure source.

```text
Pitot (total) ─────► ASI diaphragm
Static port ───────► ASI case + altimeter + VSI
ASI indication ∝ (total pressure − static pressure)
```

### Blockage / failure consequences

| Fault | Likely indication (traditional instrument) |
|---|---|
| Pitot inlet blocked, drain open | Pitot pressure drains away; ASI tends toward zero. |
| Pitot inlet **and** drain blocked; static open | Trapped pitot pressure remains; ASI behaves roughly like an altimeter: tends to rise in climb and fall in descent. |
| Static port blocked | Altimeter freezes; VSI returns to zero after its transient; ASI becomes erroneous (often underreads in climb and overreads in descent). |
| Static leak/position error | Altimeter/VSI/ASI can be inaccurate; compare instruments and use approved failure checklist. |
| Pitot/static icing | Suspect blocked pressure source; use pitot heat and POH procedure if installed. Do not diagnose from one indication alone. |

**Exam method:** first identify which pressure is lost/trapped, then ask what the instrument compares. VSI needs a changing static signal; a blocked static line removes the proper change.

### Airspeed definitions and estimates

| Speed | Definition / correction |
|---|---|
| IAS | Direct ASI indication, before corrections. |
| CAS | IAS corrected for instrument and position error. |
| EAS | CAS corrected for compressibility effects. |
| TAS | Speed through surrounding air; EAS corrected for density. |
| GS | Speed over ground; TAS combined with wind vector. |

- Rough exam rule: **TAS ≈ CAS + 2% per 1,000 ft** of altitude. At 5,000 ft, add roughly 10%: 110 kt TAS corresponds to about 100 kt CAS/ASI (approximation; use charts for precision).
- Wind changes **groundspeed**, not the ASI’s measurement of airflow through the air mass. A headwind does not directly reduce IAS at the same TAS/air density.
- A cover/adhesive film that disturbs or masks pressure ports can cause position/pressure error and an erroneous ASI reading.
- If ASI is unserviceable, the bank answer is **do not operate until it is fully functional**; GPS speed is not an approved replacement for required airspeed indication.

### Airspeed-indicator arcs and limits

| Marking | Meaning |
|---|---|
| White arc | Flap operating range (approximately VSO to VFE). |
| Green arc | Normal operating range (approximately VS1 to VNO). |
| Yellow arc | Caution range, normally smooth air only (VNO to VNE). |
| Red line at top | VNE: never-exceed speed. |
| Red line / limit marking | Operational limitation; do not exceed. |

V-speeds and exact arc endpoints are aircraft-specific; read the actual placard/POH. `PFP-008` figure’s red line at the end of yellow arc identifies **VNE**, not VFE/VNO.

## 9. Altimeter, altitude references and VSI

### Pressure settings and altitude terms

| Setting / term | Meaning / indication |
|---|---|
| QNH | Sea-level-reduced pressure; with QNH set, altimeter indicates altitude above MSL (subject to errors). |
| QFE | Pressure at a reference datum, commonly aerodrome/runway; with QFE set, reads height above that datum; on the datum it reads zero. |
| QNE / standard setting | Set 1013.25 hPa (29.92 inHg); altimeter reads pressure altitude / flight levels above standard datum. |
| Pressure altitude | Altimeter reading with 1013.25 hPa set. A flight level is a pressure-altitude surface. |
| Indicated altitude | What the altimeter reads at the selected subscale setting. |
| True altitude | Actual altitude above MSL, corrected for non-standard temperature (and pressure reference as applicable). |
| Height | Vertical distance above a specified surface/ground/datum. |
| Elevation | Height of a point on the earth above MSL. |

- Altimeter measures **static pressure**; subscale sets the pressure reference/datum, not an instrument or temperature correction.
- Adjust QNH before each flight and when required during cross-country flight / when passing to a new pressure reference. Use current official pressure and operating procedures.
- Pressure-setting change: approximately **1 hPa ≈ 8 m ≈ 27 ft** (lesson rounds to 30 ft). Thus +10 hPa gives about +80 m / +270–300 ft indicated. Bank’s choice: **80 m more**.
- Raising subscale pressure makes indicated altitude higher; setting a pressure higher than actual can make the altimeter over-read and place the aircraft closer to terrain than believed. “High to low, look out below.”
- Non-standard temperature: in colder-than-ISA air, true altitude is **lower** than indicated (“from hot to cold, look out below”); in warmer air, true altitude is higher than indicated. Altimeters have no temperature subscale correction.
- At ISA temperature with correct QNH, indicated altitude is approximately true altitude.
- Hysteresis is mechanical lag/history error in a pressure altimeter; pressure/system/position errors, leaks, contamination and blockage can also matter.
- Lesson conversions: 1 hPa = 1 mbar; 1013 hPa ≈ 29.92 inHg. The slide’s 700 hPa ≈ 10,000 ft; 500 hPa ≈ 18,000 ft; 300 hPa ≈ 30,000 ft are rough ISA reference values, not precise pressure-altitude calculations.

### Vertical speed indicator (VSI/variometer)

- VSI compares **present static pressure** with static pressure reaching the instrument through a calibrated restriction after a short delay (equivalently, current versus slightly lagged pressure). It senses pressure change over time, not dynamic pressure or vertical acceleration.
- Climb: outside static pressure falls; descent: it rises. VSI indication lags because of the restriction; use it to confirm trend, not as an instantaneous precision altitude source.
- Bank-specific fault: an equalising/reservoir tank that is **too big** gives an indication **too high**. (This is the keyed response; the exact error depends on the instrument design.)

## 10. Magnetic compass

### Earth field terms and compass errors

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Variation (declination) | Angle between true north and magnetic north at a location; geographic field property. |
| Deviation | Error caused by aircraft magnetic fields (steel, current, electronics, equipment); corrected by compass compensation/swing and correction card. |
| Inclination / dip | Angle between Earth’s magnetic field lines and the horizontal plane; greatest near magnetic poles, least at magnetic equator. Causes dip-related errors. |
| Oscillation | Compass card swings in turbulence/turns; liquid damps motion. |

- Electronic devices on board can influence the direct-reading compass. Keep magnetic interference in mind; use the compass correction card and cross-check with a directional gyro.
- Distinguish variation from deviation: **variation = Earth/location**, **deviation = aircraft**.
- Direct-reading compass has turning and acceleration errors in addition to deviation/variation effects. Gyro heading indicator is steadier but drifts and needs periodic alignment with the compass.

### Northern Hemisphere turning / acceleration rules

- Turning error mnemonic **UNOS** = **U**ndershoot **N**orth, **O**vershoot **S**outh. In the Northern Hemisphere, compass indication lags while turning toward north and leads while turning toward south; roll out early for north, late for south. Error is greatest near north/south headings and small near east/west.
- For a turn from 030° toward 180° (southerly), overshoot the indicated south heading: bank’s answer is about **210°** indicated.
- For the bank’s 360° to 270° item, answer **270°** indicated (follow the actual turn geometry and the answer key; do not apply a blanket 30° correction).
- Acceleration/deceleration error is strongest on east/west headings in the Northern Hemisphere: acceleration produces apparent turn toward north, deceleration toward south. Lesson mnemonic **ANDS**: accelerate → north, decelerate → south.
- Dip is caused by inclination of the Earth’s field, not by aircraft electrical deviation.

## 11. Gyroscopic and integrated flight instruments

### Gyro principles and sources

- **Rigidity in space:** a spinning gyro tends to keep its axis fixed in space; more rotor mass/speed gives greater angular momentum.
- **Precession:** a force applied to a spinning gyro produces a response about **90° later in the direction of rotation** (bank question: 90° offset). Use the diagram’s spin direction to determine which way the response goes.
- Gyro errors from lesson: parasitic drift (friction, bearings, imbalance, airflow), apparent drift from Earth rotation, drift from aircraft movement/transport, gimbal lock and tumbling. Avoid gimbal lock; severe attitude changes can tumble some gyros.
- Lesson quotes apparent drift as about **12°/hour**; actual Earth-rate component depends on latitude and is often taught as up to about 15°/hour at the poles, zero at the equator. Treat the slide’s 12°/h as an approximation, not a universal constant.

### Instruments and what each tells you

| Instrument | Basic mechanism / information |
|---|---|
| Attitude indicator / artificial horizon | Vertical-axis gyro; shows pitch and bank attitude relative to horizon. Traditional units may be vacuum driven; some are electric. |
| Directional gyro / heading indicator (DG/HI) | Horizontal-axis gyro; provides stable heading reference but drifts, so periodically set from magnetic compass. |
| Turn-and-slip / turn-and-bank | Rate gyro indicates rate/direction of turn; inclinometer ball indicates slip/skid (apparent vertical), not bank angle. |
| Turn coordinator | Canted rate gyro senses roll-in as well as turn rate; miniature aeroplane shows turn direction/rate. Ball shows coordination. |
| HSI | Horizontal Situation Indicator = directional gyro/heading information integrated with VHF navigation receiver course/deviation information. |
| ADI | Attitude Director Indicator combines attitude indicator with flight-director command cues. |
| Slip/skid ball | Ball is apparent vertical; centre it for coordinated flight. “Step on the ball” with rudder; bank item with ball to right in a right turn says reduce bank and increase turn rate to recenter. |

- Standard-rate **two-minute turn** means 360° in 2 minutes = 3°/s. Bank required depends chiefly on **TAS** (at higher TAS, more bank is needed for the same turn rate), not IAS/CAS/groundspeed. Approximate small-aircraft rule beyond bank: bank angle ≈ TAS/10 + 7° (TAS in knots); POH/flight technique takes precedence.

## 12. Engine, system and status annunciations

- Pressure-measuring instruments in the bank list: oil pressure, fuel pressure, manifold pressure, altimeter, VSI, ASI and suction gauge. (A pressure-sensitive instrument need not be an engine gauge.)
- Temperature-measuring examples: oil temperature, coolant temperature if fitted, CHT, EGT, OAT and cabin temperature.
- Typical alert colours: **red = limit/warning requiring immediate attention**, **amber/orange = caution; corrective action may be needed**, **green = normal/within range**. Blue is commonly advisory on some displays. Follow aircraft annunciator legend.
- A **red line** on an instrument normally denotes an operational limit; coloured arcs indicate operating/caution ranges rather than interchangeable “recommended” values.
- On electrical failure, distinguish electrical from vacuum/pressure instruments and independent magnetos; identify which specific instruments are electrically powered in the aircraft.

## 13. Exam traps and figure-solving checklist

1. **Identify the requested quantity first:** load factor = lift/weight; power = watts; current = amps; resistance = ohms; pressure gauge vs temperature gauge.
2. **Trace the physical source:** pitot = total pressure; static port = ambient static; ASI needs both; altimeter/VSI only static.
3. **For blockage questions, name the failed line before predicting the pointer.** Pitot and static errors are not interchangeable.
4. **Do not mix IAS/CAS/TAS/GS.** Wind changes GS; altitude/density changes TAS relative to IAS/CAS.
5. **Compass words are easy to swap:** variation is geographic, deviation is aircraft; dip/inclination is the field angle; UNOS applies in the Northern Hemisphere.
6. **Trim tab direction is not elevator direction.** Bank’s conventional nose-up trim is tab down/elevator up; balance tab moves opposite its control.
7. **Carb heat on fixed pitch:** expect RPM down. Take-off: carb heat off per bank/POH.
8. **Constant-speed prop power sequencing:** power up = RPM then MAP; power down = MAP then RPM.
9. **Rich vs lean:** more fuel = rich. With altitude and unchanged carburettor mixture, mixture gets richer, not leaner.
10. **Read graph/photo pointers precisely:** locate arrowhead/number first; identify view (side/front/plan); compare visible geometry before selecting “high wing,” “strut-braced high wing,” T-tail, fork or blade angle.
11. **Bank-specific oddities:** Q91 says “helicopter” in a general aircraft bank; Q109’s keyed icing range is −5 to +20°C while the lesson says below +5°C in visible moisture; Q62 keys an oversized VSI equalising tank as indication too high. Memorise the bank answer and retain the operational caveats above.
12. **Always defer live operation to the POH/AFM:** temperatures, RPM drops, V-speeds, fuel grade, mixture, maintenance intervals, inspection actions and hydraulic fluid are not universal.
