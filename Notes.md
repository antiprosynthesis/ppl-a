### Fogs:
| Fog type | How it forms | Conditions that favour it | Typical setting / time | How it clears |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Radiation | Ground cools by radiation at night and chills the air in contact with it down to the dew point | Clear sky, calm or light wind, moist air (small spread), long nights | Over land, overnight into early morning; worst in valley bottoms and frost hollows | Sun warms the ground (burns off) or wind increases; overcast cloud prevents it |
| Advection | Warm, moist air moves over a colder surface and is cooled from below to the dew point | Warm moist air, a colder surface, and a moderate wind to keep the air moving | Coasts and seas (sea fog), often spring/early summer; any time of day, can persist | Change of air mass or wind, surface warming, or stronger wind lifting it into stratus |
| Orographic (hill) | Moist air is forced up a slope and cools adiabatically until it saturates | Moist air, wind, and rising ground | Hills and mountains — effectively cloud sitting on high ground | Wind drops or changes direction, or the air dries out |
| Steaming (evaporation) | Cold air moves over much warmer water; water evaporates into the cold air and recondenses | A large temperature difference: cold air over warm water | Lakes, rivers and seas on cold mornings ("Arctic sea smoke") | The air warms or moves away from the warm water |
| Frontal (precipitation) | Rain falling from a warm front evaporates into the cooler air below and saturates it | Continuous precipitation with cooler air beneath the front | Near warm and occluded fronts | The front passes and the rain stops |

The common thread: every type reaches saturation either by cooling the air to its dew point (radiation, advection, orographic, frontal) or by adding moisture to it (steaming).

### Fronts:
| Feature | Warm front | Cold front |
|---|---|---|
| Slope | Shallow and gentle (roughly 1:150) | Steep (roughly 1:50) |
| Speed | Slower-moving | Faster — it catches up the warm front, causing occlusion |
| Cloud sequence | Cirrus → cirrostratus → altostratus → nimbostratus, building far ahead | Towering cumulus and cumulonimbus in a narrow band at the front |
| Cloud character | Layered (stratiform), spread over a wide area | Convective (cumuliform), concentrated |
| Precipitation | Continuous, moderate, prolonged, well ahead of the surface front | Heavy showers, possibly thunderstorms and hail; intense but brief |
| Visibility | Poor in the rain and through the warm sector (mist, murk) | Poor in the showers, then good and clear-washed behind |
| Wind change | Veers as the front passes | Veers, often more sharply and gustily |
| Temperature | Rises (warm air replacing cold) | Falls (cold air replacing warm) |
| Pressure tendency | Falls steadily ahead, then steadies in the warm sector | Falls to a minimum at the front, then rises sharply behind |
| Weather behind it | Warm sector: mild, low stratus, drizzle, poor visibility (stable air) | Bright with cumulus, showers, good visibility, gusty wind (unstable air) |
| Main hazards | Icing in layered cloud, low cloud and poor visibility | Turbulence, thunderstorms, hail, embedded Cb |
| Duration of bad weather | Long-lasting (slow, shallow) | Short-lived but more intense (fast, steep) |

The unifying idea: a warm front lifts warm air gently up a long slope, giving widespread layered cloud and prolonged steady rain, while a cold front shoves warm air up steeply, giving a narrow, violent band of convective weather followed by clear unstable air. When the cold front catches the warm front, the two merge into an occluded front, which shows a mix of both.

### Constructions:
| Type | Structure | Does the skin carry load? | Notes / typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grid / space-frame (truss) | Welded tubular steel framework | No — skin is just a non-structural cover (fabric or thin metal) | Older / simple aircraft, fuselages; loads carried entirely by the tube lattice |
| Monocoque | Skin only, no internal frame ("single shell") | Yes — skin carries all loads | Strong but heavy and hard to repair; rare in pure form |
| Semi-monocoque | Frames + stringers + stressed skin | Yes — shared between frame and skin | Most common for metal light aircraft; good strength-to-weight |
| Sandwich / honeycomb | Two thin face layers bonded to a light core (e.g. honeycomb or foam) | Yes — faces carry bending, core resists shear | Very stiff and light; used in composite airframes, floors, control surfaces |

Quick memory hook: grid = frame does everything, monocoque = skin does everything, semi-monocoque = they split the job, sandwich = two skins glued to a light filling.

### Roll over/undershoot:

Lead/lag only applies to north-south turns. The mnemonic is UNOS — Undershoot North, Overshoot South. Turning onto north, the compass lags, so you roll out early (before reaching 360). Turning onto south, the compass leads, so you roll out late
