# PPL(A) Theory Study Guide

This guide turns the repository's example questions into a concept-first revision aid. It covers the nine question-bank subjects, highlights the kinds of distinctions and calculations the questions test, and labels additional syllabus reminders separately.

Use the guide to understand and practise the material, not as a substitute for your aircraft's POH/AFM, current regulations, your instructor, or official aeronautical information. Some question-bank answers or regulatory details may be simplified or date-sensitive; verify operational and legal requirements against the current rules and the relevant State AIP.

For rapid revision, use the companion [Quick Reference index](Quick_Reference.md) to open the nine subject cheat sheets and their detailed lesson notes (`lesson-notes/`). Their formulas and memory aids draw on [Notes.md](Notes.md).

## What the actual exam reports add

I compared the nine supplied exam reports with the matching subject banks, using the green-marked answers as the report answer key. Across 598 readable question pages, every item has an existing counterpart in the repository banks, including its keyed answer. Apparent differences are image/chart-specific versions or repeated question stems with different figures/options, so I did not add duplicate questions.

The practical study insight is that the reports test the same learning objectives through shuffled choices, diagrams, chart extracts, and aircraft-performance data. Practise explaining why an answer is correct, not just memorising its position. Give extra practice to figure-based recognition and applying charts/tables, and keep an error log grouped by subject or learning objective so a missed item leads to targeted revision.

## 1. Air Law

### Question-bank emphasis

- **Documents, licences, and responsibilities:** Know what the aircraft and pilot must carry, the purpose of the certificate of registration, certificate of airworthiness and airworthiness review certificate, and the difference between a licence's validity and a rating's recency or validity. The pilot in command remains responsible for the safe conduct of the flight.
- **VFR rules and airspace:** Questions test VMC visibility/cloud-clearance minima, airspace classes, clearances, radio requirements, and who is separated from whom. Class A is IFR-only. VFR entry, clearance, and separation expectations differ by class; do not assume that hearing ATC or receiving traffic information means you are separated from other VFR aircraft.
- **Collision avoidance and signals:** Be ready to apply right-of-way rules (including head-on, converging, and overtaking), interpret aerodrome light signals and ground markings, and recognise interception signals. A pilot must still take action to avoid collision when necessary.
- **Altimetry and transponders:** QNH indicates altitude above mean sea level; QFE indicates height above the selected datum/aerodrome; 1013.25 hPa is the standard setting used for flight levels. Memorise the emergency codes: 7500 unlawful interference, 7600 radio communication failure, 7700 emergency.
- **Aeronautical information:** Know the roles of the AIP, NOTAM, charts, and other briefings. Check current notices and local procedures before flight; a chart alone may not contain the latest restrictions.

### Supplementary syllabus coverage

Review flight-plan filing, activation, delay, closure, and overdue-aircraft procedures; special VFR and night-VFR privileges/requirements; prohibited, restricted, and dangerous areas; and the distinction between ATC clearance, traffic information, and flight information. For exact minima, licensing privileges, documents, and local procedures, use the current applicable rules and State AIP rather than memorising an unqualified number from a practice question.

### Exam approach

First identify the applicable rule set: VFR or IFR, airspace class, altitude band, and local airspace procedure. For minima questions, read both the visibility and cloud-clearance conditions. For right-of-way questions, identify the relative geometry before deciding which aircraft gives way.

## 2. Human Performance

### Question-bank emphasis

- **Oxygen and circulation:** At altitude, the percentage of oxygen in air is essentially unchanged, but falling pressure reduces oxygen partial pressure and can cause hypoxia. Carbon monoxide is especially hazardous because it binds strongly to haemoglobin and is not reliably detectable by smell.
- **Recognising impairment:** Hypoxia may impair judgement before the pilot recognises it. Hyperventilation commonly causes tingling, dizziness, and visual disturbance; symptoms can overlap, so when uncertain, treat suspected hypoxia promptly and follow the aircraft/emergency procedures.
- **Pressure effects:** Ear or sinus block (barotrauma) occurs when pressure cannot equalise; decompression sickness results from nitrogen bubbles after a pressure reduction and is a particular concern after diving. Avoid flying when illness or recent diving creates an unacceptable risk.
- **Vision and orientation:** The vestibular system can mislead during acceleration, turns, or unusual attitudes. Visual illusions can arise from runway slope, width, lighting, haze, or lack of a visible horizon. Use a disciplined instrument cross-check when visual references are unreliable.
- **Workload and judgement:** Questions cover memory, attention, stress, attitudes, and human error. Stress and fatigue can narrow attention and degrade judgement. An error may be active at the controls or latent in planning, procedures, or organisation.

### Supplementary syllabus coverage

Revise night vision and dark adaptation, visual scanning, fatigue and circadian effects, alcohol/medication/illness, workload management, situational awareness, decision-making, and practical crew/passenger communication. Recognise that personal fitness is a go/no-go decision, not something to compensate for by pressing on.

### Exam approach

Distinguish the mechanism, symptoms, and immediate response. In particular, do not confuse low oxygen with low oxygen-carrying capacity (for example, carbon monoxide), or vestibular illusion with a real aircraft manoeuvre. In flight, follow the checklist and get assistance/land as appropriate rather than relying on self-diagnosis.

## 3. Meteorology

### Question-bank emphasis

- **Atmosphere and stability:** Understand pressure, temperature, density, lapse rates, inversions, and the tropopause. In the ISA troposphere, temperature falls by about 2 °C per 1,000 ft on average; a rising parcel cools at the dry adiabatic rate until saturated, then at the lower, variable saturated rate. Stability depends on comparing the environmental lapse rate with the parcel's cooling rate.
- **Moisture and cloud:** Relative humidity, dew point, and the temperature/dew-point spread indicate how close air is to saturation. Learn cloud families and what they imply: layered cloud is commonly associated with stable lifting, while towering convective cloud signals instability and potentially severe conditions.
- **Fog and fronts:** Radiation fog favours clear skies, light wind, moist air, and overnight cooling. Advection fog forms when moist air moves over a colder surface. Warm fronts tend to bring widespread layered cloud and prolonged precipitation; cold fronts are often sharper, with showers, turbulence, and possible thunderstorms.
- **Wind and pressure:** Wind results from pressure differences and is modified by Earth's rotation and surface friction. Closely spaced isobars generally mean stronger wind. Expect local effects from terrain, valleys, coasts, and mountain/valley breezes.
- **Hazards:** Be able to recognise airframe icing conditions and distinguish clear, rime, mixed ice, and frost. Thunderstorms can involve severe turbulence, hail, icing, lightning, and strong up/downdrafts; wind shear and microbursts are hazardous near the ground.
- **Weather products:** Decode METAR/TAF groups and identify precipitation, cloud amount/base, visibility, wind, and significant changes. Know the purpose of weather charts, radar, satellite imagery, AIRMET/SIGMET, and pilot reports.

### Supplementary syllabus coverage

Practise interpreting a complete weather briefing rather than decoding isolated symbols. Consider the route, terrain, freezing level, cloud tops/bases, visibility, wind at altitude, and escape options together. Review the life cycle and hazards of a thunderstorm, mountain waves/rotors, turbulence, and the effects of temperature and pressure on aircraft performance.

### Exam approach

For a cloud or fog question, connect the process (cooling or moisture addition) to the required conditions and likely location. For fronts, compare slope, cloud sequence, precipitation, wind, visibility, and temperature change. For icing, focus on visible moisture and sub-zero temperatures, while remembering that the exact icing envelope depends on droplet size and aircraft.

## 4. Communication

### Question-bank emphasis

- **Standard phraseology:** Use the prescribed words and read back safety-critical instructions, including runway, altitude/level, heading, route, frequency, transponder code, and clearances as required. A readback is not a request for a new clearance.
- **Message priority:** Distress concerns grave and imminent danger and uses MAYDAY; urgency concerns safety but does not indicate the same immediate grave danger and uses PAN PAN. Know the information needed in each initial call and the appropriate frequency.
- **Radio technique:** Make transmissions concise, listen before transmitting, use the correct station call sign and aircraft call sign, and use phonetic spelling/numerals where needed. “Roger” acknowledges receipt; it does not mean “yes” or that an instruction is accepted.
- **Information services:** ATIS, VOLMET, and FIS provide different kinds of information. Read the latest ATIS designator and tell the controller which information was received when required.
- **Failure and blind calls:** Know when and how to make a blind transmission and the basic response to radio failure. Squawk 7600 when appropriate, maintain safe flight, and apply the published procedures for the airspace and circumstances.
- **Weather and reporting codes:** Questions include Q-codes, cloud-amount abbreviations, visibility and time transmission, and standard reports.

### Supplementary syllabus coverage

Review the phonetic alphabet, distress/urgency relay, lost-communications planning before departure, frequency changes, and the difference between a clearance, instruction, acknowledgement, and request. Local radio-failure procedures can differ; consult the current AIP and instructor guidance.

### Exam approach

Read each radio question as a communication task: who is speaking, to whom, what action is requested or instructed, and what must be read back. Do not select a phrase that implies acceptance or compliance when the pilot is only acknowledging receipt.

## 5. Principles of Flight (Aeroplane)

### Question-bank emphasis

- **Forces and airflow:** Lift and drag depend on dynamic pressure, reference area, and aerodynamic coefficients. A useful model is \(L = \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2 S C_L\); drag has the same dynamic-pressure form with \(C_D\). Angle of attack is between the relative airflow and the aerofoil chord, not the aircraft's pitch attitude.
- **Drag and speed:** Induced drag is associated with lift and is high at low speed/high lift demand; parasite drag rises strongly with speed. Total drag combines induced and parasite contributions. Ground effect reduces wingtip-vortex effects close to the surface.
- **Stall and load factor:** A stall occurs when critical angle of attack is exceeded, not at one fixed airspeed. More load factor raises stall speed; in a coordinated level turn, \(n = 1/\cos(\text{bank angle})\). Recovery starts by reducing angle of attack, then applying the aircraft-specific procedure.
- **Axes, controls, and stability:** Longitudinal axis/ailerons/roll; lateral axis/elevator/pitch; vertical axis/rudder/yaw. Stability is the tendency to return toward equilibrium after a disturbance. Centre-of-gravity position affects stability and control authority; remain within the approved envelope.
- **High-lift devices and design:** Flaps increase lift and usually drag. Slots/slats delay separation; washout and stall strips help the root stall before the tip. Differential or Frise ailerons can reduce adverse yaw.
- **Propellers and turns:** Propeller pitch, engine power, and airspeed interact; constant-speed propellers regulate RPM by changing blade pitch. In a coordinated turn, lift has both vertical and horizontal components, so the aircraft needs greater lift than in straight-and-level flight.

### Supplementary syllabus coverage

Review boundary layers, pressure distribution, centre of pressure, primary/secondary control effects, adverse yaw, slip/skid, spin versus spiral dive, and the effect of configuration on drag and performance. Know the manoeuvre and recovery limitations in the POH/AFM.

### Exam approach

Separate angle of attack from pitch attitude and airspeed. For a stall question, ask whether the critical angle is exceeded; for a turn question, account for bank/load factor. Avoid treating the lift/drag equations as a complete performance model: they are relationships, not a substitute for aircraft data.

## 6. Operational Procedures

### Question-bank emphasis

- **Wind shear and microbursts:** Rapid wind changes alter indicated airspeed and flight path, and can leave little height to recover near the ground. Avoid known hazards; if encountered, use the aircraft's approved procedure and prioritise energy/flight-path control.
- **Wake turbulence:** Vortices are strongest behind aircraft that are heavy, clean, and slow. They descend and drift with the wind. Plan the approach or departure to remain above and, where possible, upwind of the preceding aircraft's flight path; apply published spacing and avoidances.
- **Fire and smoke:** Identify the likely source and use the applicable checklist immediately. Electrical, engine, and cabin fires require different actions; do not invent a generic sequence where the POH gives one.
- **Emergency and precautionary landings:** An emergency landing is forced by an immediate situation; a precautionary landing is chosen before the situation becomes critical. Select a suitable site, brief occupants, secure the aircraft, and follow the POH.
- **Runway surface and braking:** Wet, contaminated, soft, or uneven surfaces can increase take-off/landing distance and reduce directional control. Standing water can cause hydroplaning. Use the aircraft manual's technique and performance data; do not assume normal braking is available.
- **Noise and water operations:** Noise abatement must not compromise safety. For flights beyond gliding distance from land, plan suitable survival equipment and passenger briefing; inflate life vests only after leaving the aircraft.

### Supplementary syllabus coverage

Review rejected take-off and go-around decision-making, fuel and weather diversions, preflight checks, passenger briefings, survival equipment, and abnormal/emergency checklists. Practise applying the POH rather than memorising a single sequence detached from aircraft type and conditions.

### Exam approach

For scenario questions, prioritise control of the aircraft, then identify the hazard and apply the approved procedure. In wake-turbulence or wind-shear questions, locate the aircraft relative to the preceding aircraft or wind change; the same wind shift can have different effects depending on whether it increases or decreases headwind.

## 7. Flight Performance and Planning

### Question-bank emphasis

- **Mass and balance:** Total mass is the sum of component masses. A moment is mass multiplied by its arm; centre of gravity is total moment divided by total mass. Use the correct datum and units, include the required fuel and equipment, and ensure the loading remains inside the approved envelope.
- **Fuel and reserves:** Distinguish ramp mass, take-off mass, landing mass, usable fuel, and reserve. Fuel calculations must include taxi, trip, contingency/reserve, and any required alternate or additional fuel under the applicable rules and POH. Convert units carefully; the bank uses approximate Avgas density values.
- **Performance conditions:** High density altitude (for example, high elevation, high temperature, or low pressure) reduces engine, propeller, and wing performance. A tailwind, uphill slope, soft/contaminated surface, or increased mass generally worsens take-off/landing performance; a headwind generally helps, subject to approved data.
- **Climb, descent, and range:** Read the correct POH chart using the specified mass, pressure altitude, temperature, configuration, and power. Distinguish rate of climb (vertical speed) from climb gradient (height gained per horizontal distance). Do not extrapolate chart values casually.
- **Wind and route calculations:** Resolve wind into head/tailwind and crosswind components. Use chart scale, track, groundspeed, distance, and time to calculate ETA and fuel remaining.
- **Operational planning:** Route planning questions use charts, airspace, NOTAMs, frequencies, reporting points, flight plans, and alternates. Confirm weather, terrain/obstacles, fuel availability, and diversion options.

### Supplementary syllabus coverage

Practise weight-and-balance calculations across multiple loading changes, including fuel burn, and assess both take-off and landing limits. Review take-off/landing distance corrections, climb and cruise charts, endurance versus range, and decision points for diversion. Required fuel reserves depend on applicable regulations and operation; use the current rule set rather than a universal figure.

### Exam approach

Write units beside each value and convert before combining quantities. For mass-and-balance, calculate each moment explicitly and check the final CG against the correct envelope. For performance charts, use the exact conditions and configuration stated; interpolation is appropriate only where the POH permits it.

## 8. Aircraft General Knowledge

### Question-bank emphasis

- **Structure and controls:** Recognise truss, monocoque, and semi-monocoque construction; distinguish primary controls from secondary controls such as trim and flaps. Control inputs can have secondary effects, especially rudder/aileron coordination.
- **Piston engines:** Understand the four strokes (induction, compression, power, exhaust), mixture, carburettor icing, carburettor heat, ignition, cooling, lubrication, and fuel systems. Magnetos are engine-driven ignition sources, independent of the aircraft electrical system.
- **Propellers and fuel:** Know the difference between fixed-pitch and constant-speed propellers, the effect of blade pitch, and correct fuel grade/contamination checks. Apply the POH for operation and limitations.
- **Pitot-static instruments:** The ASI uses pitot and static pressure; the altimeter and VSI use static pressure. Blockages, leaks, and incorrect pressure settings create recognisable instrument errors. The VSI normally lags because it measures a pressure change over time.
- **Gyroscopic and electronic instruments:** Understand rigidity in space and precession, and identify the power source/limitations of attitude and heading instruments. Electrical equipment can affect magnetic compass readings; compare indications and use approved checks.
- **Limits and inspection:** Airspeed colour arcs and markings represent aircraft-specific limits/ranges. Exceeding a limit or experiencing a suspected structural event requires the POH/maintenance response, not an assumption that the aircraft is undamaged.

### Supplementary syllabus coverage

Revise the electrical system, alternator/generator and battery, circuit protection, vacuum systems where fitted, landing gear and brakes, flight-instrument failures, compass errors, and icing protection. Aircraft systems vary substantially by type; use the specific aircraft's POH/AFM and placards.

### Exam approach

For an instrument question, identify which pressure or physical principle it senses before reasoning about a fault. For an engine question, trace the system involved (fuel, air, ignition, oil, cooling) and use the aircraft checklist for the actual response.

## 9. Navigation

### Question-bank emphasis

- **Earth and charts:** Latitude is angular distance north/south of the equator; longitude is east/west of Greenwich. One minute of latitude is approximately one nautical mile. Great circles are shortest routes; rhumb lines cross meridians at a constant angle. Understand the properties and scale of the chart in use.
- **Direction references:** Distinguish track/course from heading, and true, magnetic, and compass references. Apply variation and deviation with their stated signs; “east/west” rules are useful checks but signed arithmetic is safer.
- **Wind triangle:** Track plus wind correction gives heading; TAS combined with wind gives groundspeed. A headwind reduces groundspeed, a tailwind increases it, and crosswind requires a correction into wind.
- **Time, distance, and fuel:** \( \text{time} = \text{distance}/\text{groundspeed} \), with consistent units. Calculate ETA in UTC when required, convert local time carefully, and update estimates using actual groundspeed and remaining distance.
- **Dead reckoning and correction:** Use checkpoints and visual features, estimate drift, and apply the 1-in-60 rule for small errors: track error in degrees is approximately \(60 \times \text{cross-track error}/\text{distance flown}\). Recalculate the correction for the remaining route rather than blindly holding the original heading.
- **Radio navigation and GNSS:** Know the basic geometry of NDB/ADF, VOR radials, VDF, DME, and SSR. A VOR radial is a magnetic bearing from the station; a QDM is the magnetic direction to the station. GNSS provides position and track guidance but does not remove the need to monitor aircraft control, airspace, and navigation integrity.

### Supplementary syllabus coverage

Practise a complete navigation log: chart route, true course, variation, wind correction, heading, groundspeed, leg time, fuel, and UTC ETA. Review pressure/temperature effects on true altitude, radio-aid service volume and limitations, GNSS integrity warnings, and lost-position procedures.

### Exam approach

Keep each direction reference labelled throughout the calculation. Check whether a bearing is TO or FROM a station, whether a reported wind direction is the direction it comes FROM, and whether a requested time is UTC or local. Sanity-check the result: heading correction should oppose the crosswind drift, and a headwind should not increase groundspeed.

## High-value revision loop

1. Read one subject summary, then work through the corresponding question bank without looking at the marked answer.
2. For every missed question, explain why the correct option is right and why the nearest distractor is wrong.
3. Revisit formulas and memory aids in `Notes.md`, but check their assumptions and units.
4. For legal, operational, and aircraft-specific questions, confirm the answer against current rules, local AIP procedures, and the applicable POH/AFM.
