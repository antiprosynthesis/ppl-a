# Operational Procedures — Detailed Lesson Notes

These notes combine the 32-page **Operational Procedure OPP 1–2** lesson deck with all 60 items in the repository's Operational Procedures bank. They explain the underlying ideas and preserve bank-specific answer wording. The compact [complete cheat sheet](../quick-reference/60_Operational_Procedures.md) is the memorisation/retrieval aid.

> **Operational caveat:** Procedures, legal minima, aircraft switch sequences, and performance data can change and vary by aircraft and aerodrome. In flight, use the current Belgian AIP/Skeyes information, ATC instructions and the aircraft POH/AFM/checklists. Treat slide checklists as examples unless they are the approved checklist for the aircraft being flown.

**Teacher-tip inferred from the marked slide focus:** Memorise the formal definitions and PIC duties, but learn emergency actions as checklist logic rather than as an unqualified list. For each scenario, be able to state (a) what the cue means, (b) what danger follows, (c) the immediate action, and (d) what the aircraft-specific manual controls.

The lesson includes ICAO Annex 6 screenshots and selected diagrams, not a complete explanation of every international/European procedure. It links the Belgian AIP and Skeyes site for current local procedures. Use the actual AIP for local circuit, noise, runway and reporting details.

## 2. Noise abatement: minimise impact without compromising safety

### Why aircraft noise varies

Ground noise depends on the source and on how sound reaches people: engine power and propeller RPM change emitted noise; gear and flap configuration add drag/noise; ground track and height determine which people are overflown and how far the aircraft is from them. “Flight rules” are not a physical source of noise, which is why the bank's correct set is factors 1–6, excluding item 7.

### Departure choices

The bank deliberately contrasts safe route planning with a low shortcut. Near villages, climb straight ahead where safe, avoid low-altitude turns over built-up areas, circumnavigate settlements and cross them at sufficient altitude. Published noise routes and obstacle-clearance requirements govern; a noise procedure never authorises unsafe flight.

### Arrival choices

A high approach at minimum practicable power, a later descent and later configuration reduce time spent low and powered, while following established arrival tracks avoids unnecessary overflight. The answer is not “low approach,” early configuration, or a direct route whenever convenient. The lesson slide shows NADP1/NADP2-type profile diagrams; exact profiles and speeds must come from the applicable aerodrome procedure and aircraft performance limits.

## 3. Wind shear and microburst

### Concept

Wind shear is a change in wind speed and/or direction over a short distance, vertically or horizontally. It matters because airspeed and flight-path response lag as an aircraft crosses from one wind field to another. At low altitude there may be too little height to recover from a sudden loss of airspeed or downdraft.

Inversions can concentrate a wind change across a shallow layer. Thunderstorms and heavy showers can produce severe, rapidly changing winds and downdrafts; a microburst is especially dangerous close to the ground. Therefore the preventive decision is to avoid take-off/landing during a storm or heavy-shower passage, not to plan to “out-fly” the shear.

### Approach-path reasoning

- Headwind increasing: initially raises IAS and lift, tending to lift the aircraft above the intended approach path.
- Headwind decreasing (or tailwind increasing): IAS and lift fall, so the aircraft sinks below the intended path.
- Tailwind decreasing is equivalent to headwind increasing: IAS rises and the approach path tends high.

This relationship is what the bank questions test. When asked for two effects, answer both the path and IAS, not just the wind description.

### Microburst diagram (OPR-001)

The diagram shows a downdraft spreading outward near the surface. It teaches a sequence rather than a single wind shift: encounter an increasing headwind, pass through the descending core, then reach the outflow where the headwind diminishes or becomes a tailwind. The airspeed can rise at first and then fall; the strongest downdraft reduces rate of climb. The question's numbered points must be read from the location of the depicted aircraft.

The lesson's abbreviated labels are:
- Point 1: IAS increase; ROC may increase, decrease or stay the same.
- Point 2: ROC decrease; IAS approximately unchanged in the simplified figure.
- Point 3: IAS decrease; ROC decreases.

This explains the apparently paradoxical question where a strong initial IAS rise is followed by a decreased climb rate and airspeed as the microburst develops.

### Response and traps

The bank's best-answer action after inadvertent entry just after lift-off is maximum power, preserve current configuration, and climb at best rate-of-climb speed. Keeping the current configuration avoids the performance interruption caused by changing lift/drag and delays; max power and the prescribed climb speed prioritise flight-path performance. Avoid improvised turns, abrupt pitch changes, or retracting flaps/gear contrary to the approved procedure. In every actual aircraft, POH/AFM and training control the exact action.

**Exam traps:**
- Wind shear is not “only a 15-kt change” and not an Alpine downslope wind.
- A summer calm day is not the defining condition; an inversion and thunderstorms are strong cues.
- Do not confuse changing IAS with changing ground speed/TAS.
- A wind change can first improve IAS while the downdraft still degrades climb performance.

## 4. Wake turbulence and jet blast

### Formation and strength

A wing produces lower pressure above and higher pressure below. Air curls around each wingtip and forms a pair of rotating vortices. The vortex system is most intense when the generating aircraft is heavy, clean and slow: it must generate substantial lift, while the slower wake is more concentrated. Thus, for the same aircraft, weight and flap state, the slower aircraft generates stronger wake; altitude alone is not the determining variable in the bank items.

Vortices descend, persist, and drift according to wind. A light crosswind may move one vortex away while leaving the other near the runway. This is why a little crosswind after a heavy departure is not a guarantee of a clear runway.

### Operational avoidance

For take-off behind a heavier aircraft, aim to lift off before its rotation point and climb above its flight path. For landing, remain above the preceding approach path and touch down beyond its touchdown point. The bank wording specifies the touchdown point of the airliner's nose gear. Use published wake spacing/ATC; never treat the diagram's radar minima as pilot-selected separation.

Jet blast is different from wingtip wake: it is high-velocity exhaust behind a turbine aircraft. The bank's taxi question keys a 200 m minimum distance behind a commercial airliner for jet-blast avoidance. It is not a universal substitute for airport ground instructions.

### Lecture-only category table

One slide gives categories J (super), H (heavy), M (medium) and L (light) with radar wake separation values. Those values and its note on common 3-NM approach / 5-NM area radar separation are included in the cheat sheet for recognition, but are not tested by the bank and can be superseded by current rules. Students should understand that the preceding and succeeding category both matter; follow ATC rather than self-applying a remembered cell.

## 5. Emergency decision-making and landings

### Terms

The difference is the reason for landing:
- **Emergency landing:** circumstances force a landing.
- **Precautionary landing:** the pilot elects to land to preserve safety before the situation forces it.
- **Off-field/outlanding:** describes an unprepared landing site, not whether the landing is emergency or precautionary.

An emergency landing does not necessarily mean “engine without power”; the bank explicitly tests that distinction.

### Choosing a site

Assess the site before descending into it: shape and size, slope, surface, crops, obstacles, wind, approach/departure path and escape options. The bank favours harvested corn or a light-brown short-crop field, and rejects ploughed earth, long grass, ripe waving crops and a village sports area. The slide/lesson emphasis on terrain investigation means to establish the approach points and landing area rather than making a low, flat, rushed approach for ground contact.

For an uneven/bumpy surface, the bank's glider answer is minimum speed and elevator held back through the ground roll. The aim is to avoid unnecessary speed and protect the nosewheel/airframe, while using the type's actual technique.

### The Q31 sequence and priorities

The exam's keyed order is: locate a suitable area → plan the approach → complete the applicable emergency procedure → declare the emergency → start and concentrate on the approach in due time. This highlights early site/approach planning. In practice, control the aircraft first; make the distress call and perform checklist items as workload/time permit, without allowing radio work to disrupt the landing.

### Passenger briefing

The briefing should be short enough to follow under stress and include the nature of the emergency, pilot intentions, brace/safety position, exits/evacuation instructions and actions after landing. The lesson slide uses an aircraft safety briefing card with brace-position, exits, life jackets, prohibited actions and emergency equipment. Brief the actual aircraft's doors, restraints and equipment, not generic diagrams.

### Hot brakes and tyres

After a precautionary landing, hot wheels/brakes can start a fire or cause a tyre failure. The bank's hazard is a tyre bursting **axially**; approach the wheel from its front or rear, not from the side. Do not cool brakes with water or remove wheel fairings as a shortcut.

## 6. Ditching and survival over water

The central operational problem is survivability after leaving the aircraft. If a planned route is beyond gliding distance of land, carry suitable flotation for every occupant. Keep it accessible and include it in the passenger briefing. A life jacket inflated inside the cabin can obstruct movement through an exit; inflate it **after egress**.

The lesson deck shows an EASA NCO.IDE.A.175 excerpt that lists conditions for life jackets/individual flotation devices, including specified single-engine landplane operations beyond gliding distance from land and operations with likely ditching paths, seaplanes over water and certain distances from land. The displayed source is a dated regulatory extract; check the current rule and applicability. For the two bank questions, the correct practical preparation is life vests or a life raft for all occupants, not continuous ATC contact, a special transponder code or exact waypoints as a substitute.

A pictured C150/APCK ditching checklist gives one aircraft-specific example: emergency radio frequency, secure heavy objects, consider wind and swell direction, set the specified flap/approach profile, protect the face, evacuate and inflate flotation only outside. Wind/swell technique and speeds are aircraft- and condition-dependent; do not generalise this C150 card to another type.

## 7. Fire, smoke and carbon monoxide

### Why start fires occur

Pumping the throttle can introduce excess fuel into the intake during a cold start. If it ignites, a carburettor/intake fire may occur. The bank identifies this as a piston-engine hazard. Use the correct priming/start procedure; do not pump the throttle unless the POH specifically directs it.

### Choose actions by fire source

Smoke location is a clue, but not proof. A little smoke behind the instrument panel suggests an electrical fire; the first keyed response is master switch off. A cable fire question keys the same first action. By contrast, if an engine fire's smoke is entering the cockpit, the immediate keyed action is to turn off heating and ventilation; for the suspected engine fire the bank asks to turn off heat. The fire-source checklist matters—master switch off is not a universal response for every fire.

If an engine has already been on fire, do not restart it: reignition risk is too high. This is why “restart only to taxi” is wrong.

### Toxic smoke and extinguishing agents

Carbon monoxide is the bank's most dangerous gas. It is toxic and may accompany exhaust/fire smoke; smoke should never be treated as harmless. Water is the least suitable of the listed agents for aircraft fires overall. The slide's A–F chart maps common agents to ordinary solids, liquids, gases, metals, electrical equipment and cooking fats, but suitability depends on fire and device. An aircraft extinguisher label and approved procedure take precedence.

### Checklist examples in the slides

The deck reproduces C150/APCK checklists. They illustrate checklist logic:
- Electrical fire: master switch off; electrical switches except ignition off; vents/cabin air/heat off; use extinguisher; ventilate after discharge. If electrical power is later essential, restore only as directed and monitor for recurrence.
- Engine fire in flight: use the model's fuel/mixture, cabin-air, airspeed and forced-landing procedure.
- Cabin fire: isolate electrical power as directed, close air/heat, extinguish, ventilate after discharge and land as soon as practicable.
- Fire during start: follow the start-fire checklist and inspect for damage; the depicted sequence branches depending on whether the engine starts.

These are examples, not generic checklists. Switches, speeds, actions, and order differ by model; the specific aircraft POH/AFM always wins.

## 8. Contaminated runways, surface performance and aquaplaning

### Contamination definition

The bank gives a defined threshold: more than 25% of the runway surface area in the length and width being used is covered by water, slush, snow or ice more than 3 mm deep. Q56 compresses this to “25%” as the answer. Preserve both the threshold and the depth/area conditions; “any rain means contaminated” is not the exact definition. A runway below the definition can still be slippery.

The lesson labels runway-condition reporting/RwyCC but supplies no full RwyCC calculation table. Do not invent a code from a surface description; use the current runway report and performance data.

### Surface effects

Wet grass increases both take-off and landing distance in the bank. Wet snow and standing water increase rolling resistance during take-off. Contaminants reduce tyre friction and can increase take-off/landing distances, degrade braking and make directional control harder. Actual performance depends on aircraft, depth/type of contaminant, wind, temperature, slope and runway condition: use approved data.

### Aquaplaning

When a tyre rides on water, effective contact and braking can be reduced. After heavy rain the danger is a longer braking distance, not a shorter one. If hydroplaning is suspected and wheels are moving, the keyed response is moderate braking and aerodynamic directional control; maximum braking may worsen loss of control. If a contaminated runway landing is unavoidable, the bank keys minimum crosswind, maximum flap, firm touchdown at minimum safe speed, careful braking. Operationally, follow the AFM/POH and current runway report.

## 9. Flight time and other bank definitions

The keyed question defines flight time as first aircraft movement to final rest at the end of the flight. Read the choices carefully: take-off run to touchdown is airborne time, and engine start/shutdown is not the keyed period. Legal definitions can depend on aircraft/operation; use the current applicable text for logging.

## 10. Lesson extras beyond the question bank

These points are taught or referenced in the deck, but no question in this 60-item bank requires them. They are useful for understanding the broader course, not a substitute for the relevant subject lesson.

### Circuit / pattern

The lesson diagram shows departure, crosswind, downwind, base and final legs in a normal left-hand circuit, plus a right-hand circuit. A teardrop/overhead entry illustration crosses midfield 500–1,000 ft above circuit altitude, then descends and joins downwind. These are diagram conventions; actual circuit direction, height, noise-sensitive areas and joining procedure are aerodrome-specific and published in the AIP/briefing.

### Airport markings and signs

The slide's marking key distinguishes runway holding, approach/ILS critical-area holding, taxiway holding, runway location/distance remaining, taxiway direction/destination, information, no-entry and taxiway-edge markings. Read sign colour/placement and surface markings together, and stop at the required holding position unless cleared. Use current ICAO/AIP chart legends; the slide is an introductory visual key.

### Aircraft documents and PIC responsibilities

A lesson checklist groups aircraft documents (registration, airworthiness certificate, ARC, AFM/POH, radio station licence, aircraft logbook, mass-and-balance/CG report, noise certificate, insurance) and pilot documents (licence/endorsements, identity, medical, required equipment and permits). The PIC is responsible for a safe/airworthy operation and checking applicable documents, but exact carriage requirements vary by jurisdiction and operation.

### Passenger, fuel, oxygen, logbook

The slide points to passenger briefing, fuel logic/types, oxygen and logbooks as syllabus areas. Apply the approved fuel grade and quantity procedure; check passenger equipment and survival needs; use oxygen requirements from current rules/POH; maintain required records. Detailed fuel planning, human performance/oxygen, and document questions belong to their relevant study sections, not this bank's 60 questions.

### ELT, SAR, flight plans and occurrence reports

The lesson introduces ELT and SAR alert phases, flight-plan fields, and mandatory reporting. The diagram shows satellite-based distress alerting and rescue coordination. Know the general sequence—alert and locate a distressed aircraft, coordinate search/rescue, provide relevant flight-plan and equipment information—but use the current national reporting and flight-plan procedures. The slide lists Belgian reporting contacts; confirm current contacts and legal reporting thresholds before use. None of these topics is directly tested in the supplied bank.

### ICAO Annex 6 / PANS-OPS / altimeter settings / mountain flying

The deck references ICAO Annex 6 and circuit procedures but does not provide complete PANS-OPS instrument-procedure design, altimeter-setting rules, or mountain-flying techniques. None of the 60 questions tests these subjects. They are deliberately not filled in with unsourced minima or operational values here; consult the applicable Air Law/Meteorology lessons, Belgian AIP, current ICAO/EASA material and instructor.

### Bird strike

The deck and bank contain no bird-strike question or dedicated bird-strike checklist. General awareness: maintain control, avoid abrupt manoeuvres, assess aircraft/engine damage, inform ATC and land at a suitable aerodrome if damage/abnormal indications are suspected. The aircraft checklist and instructor guidance define the appropriate response.
