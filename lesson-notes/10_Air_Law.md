# Air Law — lesson notes (Luchtvaartreglementering)

These notes follow the available 19-page lesson PDF, **APCK cursus PPL luchtvaartreglementering** (Johan De Boeck), and explain the material behind the Air Law question bank. Dutch terms are included where they help identify the lesson. The lesson PDF is the only file listed in the supplied Vak5 Air Law folder; its fifth page contains no readable slide text. Images/diagrams on the remaining pages were reviewed as well as extracted slide text.

## 1. International and European rules (lesson pp. 2–4)

### Chicago Convention and ICAO

The Convention on International Civil Aviation (Chicago Convention) was signed on **7 December 1944** and laid the foundation for ICAO, which became operational in **1947**. ICAO (International Civil Aviation Organization) is a United Nations agency headquartered in Montreal. The lesson describes **193** member countries and ICAO's principal purpose as promoting safe international civil aviation through common standards.

Each State is sovereign over the airspace above its territory and territorial waters. International agreements permit civil aviation to use member States' airspace in principle, but States may impose conditions such as flight-plan filing. ICAO's technical standards and recommended practices are published as Annexes to the Convention. They do not automatically become domestic law in exactly the same way everywhere: States implement them and may notify differences. Many European and Belgian rules closely follow ICAO.

**Exam connection:** ICAO Annex 1 is Personnel Licensing. Do not confuse an ICAO Annex (international standard) with a directly binding EU regulation.

### EASA, EU regulations and SERA

The lesson describes EASA (European Aviation Safety Agency) as founded in 2002, headquartered in Cologne, with a Brussels office and 31 participating States at the time of the slides (27 EU members plus Switzerland, Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein). EASA develops/proposes technical rules and assists the European Commission with implementation and oversight.

A simplified rulemaking sequence from the lesson:

1. A **Notice of Proposed Amendment (NPA)** proposes a change and invites comments.
2. Technical material may be published as **AMC (Acceptable Means of Compliance)** and **GM (Guidance Material)** to show acceptable compliance and explain rules.
3. A European regulation is adopted through EU institutions and becomes binding in Member States without separate national ratification.

Easy Access Rules bring the regulation together with associated AMC/GM for use. The lesson references Basic Regulation 216/2008 as its historical example; regulatory framework numbers can change, so do not treat that slide's citation as a current-law lookup.

**SERA** means Standardised European Rules of the Air. It is the key European rulebook for PPL rules of the air and largely adopts ICAO-based rules. The policy context is a **Single European Sky (SES)**. For the bank, remember that EU regulations are legally binding; ICAO Annexes and EASA explanatory/AMC materials have different legal status.

### EUROCONTROL and Belgium

The slides note EUROCONTROL's cooperation with EASA, European traffic-flow management, and ATC above **FL245** over the Benelux and parts of Germany as lesson examples. This is not a substitute for checking today's sector boundaries or service provider.

Belgian responsibilities listed in the lesson:

- **FOD Mobiliteit**: federal responsibility for civil aviation.
- **DGLV** (Directoraat-Generaal Luchtvaart), the Belgian **CAA/competent authority**: supervises application of EASA rules and may impose limited additional requirements.
- **Skeyes**: national **ANSP** (Air Navigation Service Provider).
- **Flemish and Walloon regional governments**: operation, equipment and maintenance of regional airports.
- Belgian rules supplement EU rules; the slide specifically notes their application to Belgian **OO-registered** aircraft, including abroad.

**Beyond question bank:** The organizational chart on slide 5 shows how EU regulations sit under the Basic Regulation and distinguishes safety, environmental, and EASA-function regulations. The chart is a structural overview, not a list to memorise regulation-by-regulation for the bank.

## 2. Civil twilight and aircraft lighting (lesson pp. 6–8)

### Civil twilight (burgerlijke schemering)

Civil twilight occurs when the Sun's centre is no more than **6 degrees below the horizon**. Morning civil twilight runs from that point until sunrise; evening civil twilight runs from sunset until the Sun reaches 6° below. The lesson explains its importance to day/night definitions, flight-time logging, available natural light and planning a landing before darkness at an unlit aerodrome.

The slide says there may still be enough natural light for VFR during civil twilight, but navigation lights may already be required according to local rules. Treat the legal night boundary and aircraft-light requirements as distinct questions: use current national rules/AIP for operations.

### Aircraft lights shown by the lesson

The lesson groups aircraft lights as:

1. **Anti-collision lights**: rotating beacon and strobe lights.
2. **Navigation/position lights**: red, green and white.
3. **Landing and taxi lights**.

Viewed in the normal aircraft convention, red is on the left wing, green on the right wing and white is aft. These lights let others infer orientation and detect an aircraft at night. Lighting configuration/operation depends on the applicable rules and aircraft equipment.

**Beyond question bank:** The lesson's figures show beacon, wingtip/tail lights and landing/taxi beam placement. Their practical purpose is orientation, conspicuity and illumination, rather than a separate navigational clearance.

## 3. Right-of-way and collision avoidance (lesson p. 9)

The lesson presents a broad priority hierarchy based on relative manoeuvrability:

- Powered aerodynes give way to airships, motor gliders, gliders and balloons.
- Airships give way to motor gliders, gliders and balloons.
- Motor gliders and gliders give way to balloons.
- Powered aircraft give way to aircraft towing another aircraft or object.

The teacher's stated logic is that priority reflects the ability/speed to manoeuvre out of the way. Apply the specific rules as well: head-on, both turn right; in a crossing situation, the aircraft with the other on its right gives way; an overtaking aircraft keeps clear. A more manoeuvrable aircraft should not use its priority to cut across a less manoeuvrable one in a critical phase.

**Exam-bank inconsistency:** Q73's keyed answer says both engine-driven aircraft on crossing tracks divert right. That does not match the ordinary crossing give-way rule or the bank's own other questions. The cheat sheet preserves the marked answer for that exact MCQ and flags the difference; in flight, follow current SERA and instructor teaching.

## 4. PAPI and aerodrome circuit (lesson pp. 10–11)

### Precision Approach Path Indicator

PAPI (Precision Approach Path Indicator) is a **visual glidepath aid for the pilot**. Each unit shows red below its beam and white above. Four units produce a graded indication:

| Lights seen | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| 4 red | Well/too low |
| 3 red + 1 white | Slightly low |
| 2 red + 2 white | On glidepath |
| 1 red + 3 white | Slightly high |
| 4 white | Well/too high |

The image also shows the PAPI equipment on the runway side and a runway approach view. The critical exam method is to count red vs white: **red dominates = low; white dominates = high; 2/2 = on slope**.

### Circuit diagram

The lesson figure labels the standard aerodrome circuit legs:

- **Upwind**: climb after take-off, parallel and offset from the runway.
- **Crosswind**: turn away from the runway track.
- **Downwind**: parallel to the runway in the opposite direction, with the runway on the appropriate side for the circuit.
- **Base**: turn toward final.
- **Final approach**: align with the runway for landing.
- **Departure leg**: continue after take-off before turning onto crosswind.
- **Decision point**: decide whether to continue downwind or turn base, based on spacing/traffic and conditions.

The diagram shows a left-hand circuit; circuit direction is not universal. Check published/local circuit procedures, traffic, wind and ATC instructions.

**Beyond question bank:** A diagram-reading question can test leg names, relative orientation to runway, or which leg follows another. Locate the runway heading and wind first, trace the circuit arrows, then label the leg.

## 5. Airspace structure, airways and route planning (lesson pp. 12–14)

### Three-dimensional airspace

The lesson's hand-drawn cross-section shows that an airspace volume has a horizontal footprint plus a base and a top. A chart label alone is incomplete until its vertical limits are known. Examples in the diagram include:

- **CTR** (control zone): surface-based controlled area around an aerodrome.
- **TMA/CTA**: controlled volumes above/around terminal routes, with stepped bases/tops.
- **AWY**: airway route volumes.
- **FIR**: broad flight-information region.
- **G**: uncontrolled airspace in the example.
- **NG/TRA-like restricted or reserved volumes**: local published areas whose activation and restrictions matter.
- **Transition altitude / transition layer** and QNH/standard pressure references.

The 3-D slide sketches an airway crossing a TMA and a CTR below it. A route may be laterally over an airport but vertically outside its controlled volume, or the reverse; always compare both horizontal position and altitude.

### Airway and practical chart

The airway slide depicts an airway as a defined three-dimensional corridor passing through/over terminal structures, not merely a line on a map. The SkyDemon screenshot shows Belgian/nearby airspace, aerodrome symbols, NOTAM overlays and a vertical profile. Route planning therefore requires checking the route laterally **and** vertically, active NOTAMs/temporary restrictions, and weather/terrain.

**Beyond question bank:** Use the chart's legend and vertical profile; identify an airspace boundary, base, top, class and active time before deciding whether entry is permitted. The screenshot is a planning illustration, not a current chart.

## 6. Semi-circular cruising system (lesson p. 15)

The diagram explains the semi-circular system as a way to reduce the chance of opposing aircraft occupying the same cruising level. It keys off the **magnetic route/track**. The pictured compass divides headings/tracks into eastbound **000°–179°** and westbound **180°–359°**. The lesson uses odd flight levels to the east and even levels to the west; VFR levels add 500 ft.

Examples written on the lesson diagram:

- Eastbound/000°–179°: FL50, FL70, FL90, FL110 (odd thousands).
- Westbound/180°–359°: FL60, FL80, FL100, FL120 (even thousands).

A companion graphic labels the north-east/east half as odd and the west half as even. Above the applicable VFR threshold, add 500 ft to the corresponding thousand-foot level. The system is subject to published exceptions, ATC instructions, route structure and the applicable rules; it is a traffic convention, not a guarantee of separation.

## 7. Altimeter settings (lesson pp. 16–17)

### Inside/near controlled airspace

The lesson illustrates QNH below the transition altitude and standard pressure (**1013.2/1013.25 hPa**) above it. In climb, change to standard when passing **above TA**; in descent, change back to local QNH at the published **TL**. The layer between them prevents a misleading altitude/flight-level comparison when pressure settings change.

The slide's example uses **4500 ft AMSL** as a transition-altitude reference. Treat TA/TL as local published values, not a universal value to assume. A flight level is a pressure surface based on 1013.25 hPa; an altitude is referenced to mean sea level (QNH); a height is referenced to a local datum (QFE).

### Outside controlled airspace

The second sketch labels a **4500 ft AMSL** reference and a regional QNH line (annotated 3500 ft in the drawing), alongside terrain/AGL and MSL references. The teaching point is that the chosen pressure reference and cruising level depend on whether the aircraft is under a controlled-airspace procedure and on published local rules. Below transition use QNH; above transition use the standard setting and flight levels. Do not assume “uncontrolled” means “ignore altitude references.”

**How to solve the bank question:** If the aircraft is at FL80, use 1013.25 hPa. When climbing through TA, change from local QNH to standard. On descent, use the published TL to return to QNH.

**Beyond question bank:** Local TA/TL and regional QNH procedures should be learned from the current Belgian AIP and instructor briefing; handwritten diagram annotations are not a replacement for current procedures.

## 8. VFR weather minima (lesson p. 18)

The illustrated table/drawing distinguishes the altitude bands and visibility/cloud separation. It shows the broad pattern:

- Above the FL100 boundary, the bank/lesson uses **8 km** flight visibility.
- Below FL100 and above the low-level threshold, **5 km** visibility.
- In the low-level band, the drawing shows **5 km**, **1000 ft AGL** as a reference and remaining clear of cloud with the surface in sight where that category/rule applies.
- For the standard controlled-airspace diagram, clouds are separated by **1500 m horizontally** and **1000 ft vertically**.
- The lesson depicts terrain elevation, AGL/AMSL and the surface as distinct references; a visibility answer is not a cloud-clearance answer.

The question bank includes these exact prompts: Class C below FL100 = 5 km; Class E FL75 = 5 km; Class C FL110/FL125 = 8 km; Class B at 5000 ft MSL = 5 km; C/D/E cloud separation = 1500 m horizontal and 1000 ft vertical; Class B cloud separation = 1500 m horizontal and 300 m vertical. The latter is simply the rounded metric equivalent of 1000 ft (about 305 m), so units—not a substantive conflict—explain the different wording. Verify current SERA and local procedures before operations.

**Method:** read class → altitude band → flight visibility → cloud distance → any special low-level/surface-in-sight condition. Check all axes; the figure is a vertical cross-section, not a plan view.

## 9. Runway Condition Report (lesson p. 19)

The last slide shows the Runway Condition Assessment Matrix (RCAM) and surface-condition definitions. RCR reports condition for each third of the runway through the relevant AIS/ATS message. Reportable contaminants in the slide include water, snow, slush, ice, frost, anti-icing/de-icing chemicals or other contaminants, and snowbanks/drifts.

| Runway Condition Code | Surface/assessment headline in lesson |
|---:|---|
| 6 | Dry. |
| 5 | Good: e.g. wet (no significant standing water), frost, slush up to 3 mm, dry snow up to 3 mm, wet snow up to 3 mm. |
| 4 | Specially prepared winter runway / compacted snow below −15 °C; good to medium. |
| 3 | Medium: braking/control noticeably reduced; e.g. slippery wet, snow/slush over 3 mm, compacted snow warmer than −15 °C. |
| 2 | Medium to poor: standing water over 3 mm or slush over 3 mm. |
| 1 | Poor: ice. |
| 0 | Less than poor: wet ice or water over compacted snow / dry or wet snow over ice. |

The three bank-tested surface terms:

- **DAMP**: moisture changes the surface colour.
- **WET**: surface is soaked but has no significant standing-water patches.
- **WATER PATCHES**: patches of standing water are visible.

Do not infer aircraft braking performance from the word alone; use the complete, current RCR and the aircraft's performance information.

## 10. Lesson-to-bank review and content beyond the bank

The available lesson material supplements the question bank with: the legal hierarchy and organizations; civil twilight; aircraft-light purpose/configuration; priority logic; PAPI indications; circuit legs; 3-D airspace/AWY diagrams; practical chart and vertical-profile reading; cruising-level examples; transition-layer concepts; and RCAM/RCR detail. These concepts are included in the cheat sheet or explained here as **beyond question bank** where the bank does not test the detail.

All 94 bank questions are represented in the quick reference. Two source oddities are explicitly flagged rather than silently reconciled: Q5 has a truncated question stem, and Q73's marked crossing answer conflicts with the ordinary give-way principle and the bank's own other crossing questions. The Class B cloud vertical distances are unit-equivalent: the bank says 300 m and the lesson says 1000 ft. Use the bank key for its multiple-choice item; use current authoritative rules for actual flight.
