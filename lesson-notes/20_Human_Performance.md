# Human Performance & Limitations — Detailed Lesson Notes

Detailed English-language notes based on the supplied Human Performance course material: *HF.pdf*, *HumanPerf1.pdf*, *HumanPerf2.pdf*, *cursus mensen en hun grenzen* (109-page Dutch course book), the instructor’s topic outline, and the two supplementary question PDFs. Dutch terms are included where they help connect the lesson to class. Items marked **Beyond question bank** extend beyond the 60 questions in `20_Human_Performance.md`.

> **Exam priority:** where a lesson source and the supplied question bank conflict, learn the bank’s marked answer for the exam, while retaining the lesson context and checking local instructor guidance. Avoid turning a classroom simplification or old rule into operational advice.

## Lesson 1 — What Human Factors is for

Human Factors brings together psychology, ergonomics, physiology, decision-making, workload, situational awareness and stress management. The course presents the field as a response to the recognition that many accidents have a human contribution; the focus is not blaming individuals but understanding limitations and designing better defences.

The teacher’s introductory example is the 1977 Tenerife accident. It illustrates how communication, authority gradients, expectations, workload, weather and decision-making interact; accidents are rarely explained by one isolated mistake. Crew Resource Management (CRM) later developed as a way to use people, procedures and available resources effectively. In a single-pilot aircraft, CRM concepts still apply to passenger assistance, ATC, checklists and asking for help.

**Learning point:** flying makes physical and cognitive demands that are unlike ordinary life. The pilot has an ongoing personal responsibility to be fit, maintain margins and stop a chain of unsafe decisions early.

## Lesson 2 — Atmosphere and gas laws

### Atmosphere / ISA

The course gives the atmosphere’s approximate composition as 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen and 1% other gases. Its ISA reference values are sea-level pressure 1,013.25 hPa, sea-level temperature 15°C and density 1.225 kg/m³. Temperature and pressure generally fall with altitude in the troposphere; the lesson gives a tropopause around 11 km and −56.5°C. The important physiological idea is that oxygen’s *fraction* stays near 21%, while falling total pressure reduces its partial pressure.

### Boyle, Charles, Dalton and Henry

1. **Boyle’s law:** for a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pressure × volume is constant. Lower external pressure expands trapped gas. At about 18,000 ft pressure is roughly half sea-level pressure, so a dry trapped gas volume is roughly doubled. Moist gas in the digestive tract can expand even more at a given altitude. Examples include gut discomfort, ear / sinus / dental pain and cabin or medical gas spaces.
2. **Charles’ law:** at constant pressure a gas volume changes directly with absolute temperature. Always use kelvin when doing a numerical calculation.
3. **Dalton’s law:** total pressure is the sum of the component gases’ partial pressures. Oxygen’s share is about 21%; thus its partial pressure falls with total atmospheric pressure, reducing the pressure gradient that moves O₂ from the lungs to blood.
4. **Henry’s law:** the amount of gas dissolved in a liquid is related to its partial pressure. When pressure falls, dissolved gas may leave solution. The textbook compares this with bubbles escaping when a fizzy drink is opened; nitrogen bubbles in tissue are the basis of decompression sickness.

**Teacher’s tip:** do not say that the air “contains less than 21% oxygen” at altitude. The mixture fraction remains essentially constant; pressure and oxygen availability per breath fall.

## Lesson 3 — Trapped gas and pressure changes

As a non-pressurized aircraft climbs, trapped gas in the gut, sinuses, middle ear and sometimes beneath a dental filling expands. Pain can distract the pilot. During descent, pressure rises again; if a connection is blocked, pressure equalisation can fail. The course suggests ascending again if severe pressure pain develops, rather than forcing a painful descent, and arranging medical or dental attention after landing.

The Eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the nose/throat region. A cold can obstruct it, especially on descent. A blocked tube prevents equalisation; sinus blockage can also cause intense pain. The teaching material recommends ensuring a pilot can clear their ears and avoiding flight with a significant cold. Do not force a Valsalva manoeuvre against severe pain.

**Beyond question bank:** dental “barodontalgia” is possible when trapped air beneath a filling expands; the source recommends prompt descent and a dentist. This is useful background but is not one of the 60 bank questions.

## Lesson 4 — Dissolved gas and decompression sickness

At high ambient pressure (e.g. underwater), more nitrogen dissolves in body tissues. A rapid reduction in pressure can allow bubbles to form before the nitrogen is safely eliminated through the lungs. Bubbles in joints produce “the bends”; skin itching is called “the creeps”; breathing problems are called “the chokes.” Neurological signs, visual changes, coordination problems, weakness, confusion, shock or severe breathing problems are emergencies.

Do not rub or vigorously move a painful joint; the source advises remaining still, descending / recompressing as appropriate, using oxygen and obtaining medical care. Even if symptoms improve after descent, the source recommends medical assessment and observation because symptoms can recur or worsen after landing.

Scuba diving before flight is a major risk because the diver may still have excess dissolved nitrogen. The older textbook includes waiting-time examples for different dive profiles (12 h, 24 h, and 24–48 h depending on dive type) and recommends a larger margin where possible. These are historical course figures, not a replacement for current approved diving/aviation guidance.

**Exam distinction:** diving before a flight is a DCS risk (bank Q56), but the bank’s “NOT a risk factor for hypoxia” answer is diving (Q50). Do not combine decompression sickness and hypoxia.

## Lesson 5 — Hypoxia and oxygen transport

### How oxygen reaches tissues

Air passes through the upper airways, where it is warmed, filtered and humidified, then through the trachea and bronchi to the lungs. In the alveoli, oxygen diffuses across a very thin membrane into the capillaries. Blood carries oxygen to tissues; cells use oxygen to release energy from nutrients. Carbon dioxide returns to the lungs for exhalation. The heart is the pump, blood the transport medium, and vessels the pathways.

Red cells carry haemoglobin and oxygen; white cells defend against infection; platelets support clotting. The lesson notes that red cells also transport much of the carbon dioxide generated by metabolism. Anaemia, blood loss or reduced haemoglobin decreases oxygen-carrying capacity.

### CO poisoning

Carbon monoxide from incomplete combustion can enter the cabin through a cracked heat exchanger / faulty cabin heater. Smoking also exposes the person to CO. CO binds to haemoglobin much more strongly than oxygen, occupying sites that would otherwise transport O₂. This creates **anaemic hypoxia**. CO is difficult to detect by smell; headache, dizziness, nausea, drowsiness, confusion and collapse may occur, with effects worsened at altitude. The practical lesson is to stop the source (e.g. heater off), ventilate / use oxygen as directed, land promptly and seek medical care.

### Types of hypoxia

The supplied bank expressly tests anaemic hypoxia and altitude hypoxia. The wider lesson commonly distinguishes four mechanisms:

- **Hypoxic / hypobaric:** insufficient oxygen pressure in the lungs, especially at altitude.
- **Anaemic:** inadequate blood capacity to carry oxygen (CO, anaemia, blood loss).
- **Stagnant / circulatory:** oxygenated blood does not flow adequately to tissues (e.g. severe circulatory impairment or sustained G effects).
- **Histotoxic:** cells cannot use oxygen effectively despite its delivery (some toxins / substances).

### Symptoms and response

Hypoxia can be subtle. Early symptoms can include faster / deeper breathing, headache, fatigue, sleepiness, euphoria, poor judgement, reduced concentration, impaired coordination and visual change; cyanosis (blue lips or nail beds) may appear as it becomes more severe. Individual responses vary, and impaired judgement can prevent the pilot from recognizing the problem.

The course and bank emphasize that vision is particularly sensitive to hypoxia. A low oxygen state can also worsen night vision. Smoking, anaemia, blood loss / menstruation, illness, fatigue, alcohol, drugs, rapid pressure loss, high altitude and duration can increase vulnerability. The course text states that a healthy person compensates for some altitude effect but is not immune.

**Action principle:** do not debate a diagnosis while hypoxia is plausible. Use oxygen if available and appropriate, descend to a safer altitude, keep the aircraft controlled and land / seek assistance as needed. The course text includes historical oxygen-use thresholds; operational oxygen requirements vary by applicable rules and aircraft, so use current regulations rather than an old textbook summary. **Beyond question bank:** the book suggests oxygen within 30 minutes at 10,000–13,000 ft and immediately at 13,000 ft; treat this as the source’s historical teaching summary, not current legal advice. The supplementary practice sheet describes useful consciousness after decompression as seconds to minutes; the actual interval varies, so the safe response is immediate oxygen and descent, not timing a delay.

### Hypoxia vs hyperventilation

Hyperventilation means breathing too rapidly and/or deeply, often due to anxiety or stress. Excess CO₂ is exhaled; the resulting chemical changes can cause dizziness, tingling in fingers / toes / around the mouth, muscle cramps or spasm, weakness, dry mouth and altered consciousness. Cyanosis is not the expected hyperventilation symptom in the bank. Controlled slower breathing may help. Since hypoxia and hyperventilation can overlap and hypoxia can be dangerous, first check oxygenation / altitude and treat possible hypoxia rather than assuming symptoms are harmless.

**Beyond question bank:** the book distinguishes physiological hyperventilation, where the body is compensating for a real need, from non-physiological / stress-driven overbreathing. It explains the role of CO₂ in maintaining blood acidity (pH). Avoid paper-bag breathing as a general operational remedy; ensure adequate oxygen and use current medical advice.

## Lesson 6 — Circulation, blood pressure and G-force

The heart circulates blood through the lungs and body. The course notes typical resting blood pressure around 120/80 mmHg; blood vessels and reflexes help maintain blood flow to the brain. Under positive longitudinal G (`+Gz`), blood shifts toward the lower body. The eyes and brain can become under-supplied before the pilot consciously recognizes the effect.

In a coordinated level turn, load factor rises as bank increases. The course relates load to `n = 1/cos(bank)`. At 60° bank, the load is 2 G. Sustained positive G can cause peripheral tunnel vision, grey-out (loss of colour), blackout and unconsciousness. Negative G pushes blood toward the head and can produce red-out / eye discomfort. The exact tolerance depends on direction, magnitude, duration, health, fitness, protection and training.

**Beyond question bank:** the instructor slides cite +3.5 G and −3 G as long-duration tolerance examples, +7 to +8 G with anti-G equipment, and short impact values of 25 G in the vertical and 45 G perpendicular to it. These are not light-aircraft operating limits. The practical PPL response is to avoid abrupt / unnecessary G and remain within aircraft limitations.

## Lesson 7 — Nervous system and reflexes

The central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) receives sensory information, processes it and issues responses. The peripheral nervous system connects it with the rest of the body. The autonomic nervous system controls involuntary functions such as breathing, heart rate and digestion. Reflexes are stereotyped responses to receptor stimulation; some can be consciously resisted, while many essential regulation mechanisms operate without awareness.

Hypoxia, alcohol, fatigue, stress and medication degrade nervous-system performance. The pilot should avoid treating a false sensation as objective information; instrument cross-check and disciplined procedures are essential.

## Lesson 8 — Vision and visual illusions

### How vision works

Light enters through the cornea and pupil; the iris controls pupil opening; the lens focuses light on the retina. The retina sends signals toward the brain, which interprets the scene. The lesson describes about five million cones and 120 million rods. Cones cluster centrally and support detail / colour; rods are more peripheral and support dim-light sensitivity and movement detection. The blind spot occurs where the optic nerve and vessels leave the eye. The yellow spot / fovea provides fine central vision.

The **visual field** is the area seen with head and eyes still; the **view / gaze field** expands as the eyes scan. Peripheral vision is less detailed but important for movement and orientation. A systematic scan prevents “tunnel vision” from task fixation.

At night, weak light may be easier to detect peripherally than by staring directly at it. Dark adaptation takes time and is disrupted by bright light. Keep cockpit lighting low and use a considered scan; red cockpit light may preserve adaptation but can distort colours on charts, so the course suggests a weak white light when reading colour-coded material.

**Beyond question bank:** the older source mentions roughly 1–2 hours for full dark adaptation and about 20–30 minutes to recover after a bright flash. These are teaching approximations, not exam-bank facts.

### Correctable visual conditions

- Myopia / near-sightedness (bijziendheid): distant objects blur; concave lenses correct it.
- Hyperopia / far-sightedness (verziendheid): near focus is harder; convex lenses correct it.
- Accommodation is lens focusing for different distances; it decreases with age.
- Glaucoma involves raised eye pressure / damage risk; cataract clouds the lens; colour-vision deficiency can affect aviation tasks.

Parallax is not a visual illusion of attitude: it is an instrument-reading error from viewing a scale off-axis. Read analogue instruments face-on. Pilot sunglasses should be non-polarised and have suitable UV protection.

### Visual illusions

The eye supplies information, but the brain interprets it and can be misled by context, expectation, missing reference points and geometry. Examples covered in the materials:

- Runway width: a narrow runway may seem too high / close, encouraging a low approach; a wide runway may seem too low / far, encouraging a high approach.
- Runway slope: an upslope may appear too high, encouraging descent below the normal glidepath; a downslope may appear too low, encouraging a high approach.
- Lack of ground texture, haze, water, snow or desert deprives the pilot of reliable height cues.
- A single stationary light in darkness can seem to move (autokinesis). Avoid prolonged staring.
- False horizon: sloping cloud, shoreline, stars or lights can be mistaken for the horizon.
- Black-hole approach: an illuminated runway in dark surroundings can distort height / glidepath perception.
- Flicker vertigo: repeated light flashes, such as sunlight through propeller or strobes on cloud, can induce discomfort or disorientation.
- Target fixation: attention locks onto an object and the pilot stops monitoring aircraft path / instruments.

**Practical defence:** study destination details beforehand, use a stabilized approach, maintain airspeed and glidepath, scan instruments and avoid chasing a compelling visual impression.

**Bank discrepancy to note:** Q21’s marked choice is “overshoot” for an upslope runway, while Q54 states the perceived-too-high / descent-below-glidepath reaction. Record the expected bank answer for each exact wording; do not improvise the contradictory geometry in an exam. In real flight follow procedures and a stabilized approach.

## Lesson 9 — Hearing, middle ear and vestibular balance

### Hearing and pressure

The outer ear collects sound; the eardrum and middle-ear bones transmit vibration; the cochlea in the inner ear converts vibration to nerve signals. Hearing damage can be permanent and can compromise radio communication. A headset / hearing protection helps reduce noise exposure.

The Eustachian tube equalizes pressure across the eardrum. A blockage from a cold can cause pressure pain, muffled hearing or vertigo, especially in descent. Sinuses can also become painful when blocked. A significant cold is a reason not to fly.

### Balance organs

Semicircular canals contain fluid and sense angular acceleration. Their fluid lags a turn initially; with a sustained turn the sensation can fade, even though the aircraft is still turning. Otolith organs sense linear acceleration and gravity / tilt. They cannot reliably distinguish gravity from an aircraft acceleration vector. Vision usually provides the dominant orientation reference; with poor visual cues, vestibular illusions become more likely.

- **Leans:** slow roll falls below detection threshold; later correction feels like a turn the other way. A pilot may re-enter the unnoticed bank if relying on sensation.
- **Coriolis illusion:** moving the head while turning stimulates another canal plane, creating a powerful tumbling / vertigo sensation. Keep the head still during turns.
- **Somatogravic illusion:** acceleration may feel like pitch-up / climb; deceleration may feel like pitch-down / descent. Memorize: accelerate up, decelerate down.
- **Centrifugal / somatogravic illusion:** in a sustained turn, the combined gravity and acceleration vector can feel like true vertical; if the pilot trusts it instead of instruments, a descending spiral may develop.
- **Inversion illusion:** after a climb followed by rapid leveling, otolith signals may feel as if the aircraft has inverted; pushing forward in response can worsen the situation.
- **Motion sickness:** sensory conflict may produce pallor, sweating, yawning, sleepiness, nausea, hyperventilation or vomiting. Turbulence, alcohol and head movement in turns aggravate it; smooth straight flight is less provocative.

- **Disorientation types:** the instructor slides distinguish unrecognized, recognized and life-threatening disorientation; the course book focuses especially on unrecognized (Type I) versus recognized (Type II). Unrecognized disorientation is particularly dangerous because the pilot may not know the aircraft state is wrong.

**Disorientation prevention / recovery:** maintain instrument proficiency; avoid flight into deteriorating weather without skills; keep the head still in turns; avoid illness, fatigue and alcohol; if disoriented, explicitly trust instruments, stabilize and request assistance. It is not a personal weakness: a healthy sensory system can be misled.

## Lesson 10 — Health, substances and fitness to fly

### I’M SAFE

The personal check is **Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Emotion**. If a factor compromises performance, postpone or seek appropriate medical / instructor advice. A medical certificate is not a guarantee of fitness on a specific day.

### Medication, alcohol, caffeine, smoking and drugs

- Some medicines impair alertness, reaction, coordination or judgement; antihistamines are an example emphasized in the supplemental question sheet. A medicine’s warning label is not the only evidence of impairment. Ask a qualified clinician / aviation medical examiner and do not self-clear based on a general waiting period.
- Alcohol reduces judgement, attention, memory and reaction even when the person feels confident. The supplied bank asks the approximate adult blood-alcohol reduction: **0.01% per hour**. This varies substantially and is not a safe-flight calculation.
- The slide deck mentions “12 hours between bottle and throttle”; the older course book says avoid alcohol from 24 hours before flight. They conflict, and both are historical teaching statements. Follow current law, medical guidance and school policy; more importantly, do not fly impaired or hung over.
- Caffeine / nicotine may feel stimulating but can cause nervousness, palpitations or sleep disruption when overused. Smoking exposes the pilot to CO and can impair oxygen transport / night vision.
- Illicit drugs and toxic substances impair central or autonomic function, balance or judgement; they are incompatible with flight.

### Other health factors

The course discusses fatigue, sleep, illness, dehydration, diabetes, blood pressure and nutrition. Overweight is a risk factor for diabetes in both bank questions. The pilot should consider physical and psychological state together, not just a single diagnosis.

## Lesson 11 — Information processing, attention and memory

Information flows from sensory inputs through attention, perception, recognition and interpretation, then supports a decision and action. The senses have thresholds and can adapt to prolonged input. The brain selects some signals and ignores others; this is useful but creates blind spots. Information that conflicts with expectation may be overlooked.

Memory types in the course:

- **Short-term memory:** brief holding of a limited number of items; information fades unless rehearsed.
- **Long-term memory:** accumulated knowledge / experience; strengthened by repetition and meaning.
- **Working memory:** holds information while it is being processed (e.g. following a checklist or solving a task).

Chunking, mnemonics, written notes, checklists, readbacks and repetition reduce memory load. Do not rely on remembering a long radio message or several tasks while troubleshooting.

**Conflict to flag:** the supplied main question bank says short-term memory stores **5 ± 2 items for 10–20 seconds** (Q26). The supplementary ECQB-PPL excerpt instead says **7 ± 2 items for 10–20 seconds**, while the local lesson slides describe a few items for up to about 30 seconds. For this exam, use the primary supplied bank’s **5 ± 2 / 10–20 s** response; ask the instructor which capacity model is used in their broader teaching.

### Attention, expectation and confirmation bias

Selective attention can produce task fixation: a pilot focuses on one threat and misses fuel, traffic or aircraft state. Confirmation bias leads someone to seek evidence that confirms an initial interpretation and discount evidence against it. To resist it: state what evidence would disprove your hypothesis, cross-check instruments / sources, ask another person and revisit the situation when new information appears.

## Lesson 12 — Decision-making and actions

Good decisions account for the pilot, aircraft and environment; the instructor’s material adds time pressure and other resources as important contextual factors. Evaluate experience, health and workload; aircraft capability and status; weather, terrain and airspace; and time / fuel options.

The book describes a chain of poor decisions: schedule pressure or “get-there-itis” leads to overestimating ability, entering worsening weather, and continuing rather than diverting. Every early decision to continue can remove a later safe option. A go-around, diversion, delay or cancellation is a successful decision when it preserves safety.

Three behaviour levels:

1. **Skill-based:** learned automatic action (e.g. routine control input). Efficient, but a “slip” can occur when attention is elsewhere.
2. **Rule-based:** recognize a familiar situation and apply a learned rule / procedure.
3. **Knowledge-based:** unfamiliar problem requires analysis and a new solution; higher workload and error potential.

The appropriate level depends on the situation. Checklists help catch omissions in routine actions; they do not substitute for understanding or judgement.

## Lesson 13 — Attitudes, error chains and safety defences

Common hazardous attitudes and antidotes:

- Anti-authority: “rules don’t apply” → rules exist for safety; follow them.
- Impulsivity: “act now” → pause and think.
- Invulnerability: “it won’t happen to me” → everyone can be affected.
- Macho: “I can prove it / impress them” → avoid needless risk.
- Resignation: “nothing I do matters” → identify one useful action and seek help.
- Complacency: “automation / experience will handle it” → monitor and verify.

Macho and invulnerability often pair; risky manoeuvres to impress spectators are a clear macho cue. Group dynamics can create a **risky shift**, in which members accept greater risk together than they would individually. Use objective limits set before social pressure arises.

A **latent error** is a hidden system condition that may remain dormant; an **active error** occurs at the point of action. The Swiss-cheese model explains how holes across several defences can line up to permit an accident. Procedures, checklists, training, maintenance, briefings, monitoring and reporting are independent layers that can interrupt the chain.

Errors are a normal human possibility; safety aims to anticipate, detect and recover from them. A “just culture” distinguishes honest, reportable error from reckless conduct and supports learning.

## Lesson 14 — Situational awareness, communication and workload

Situational awareness is continuous: perceive what is happening, understand its significance and anticipate what is likely next. It can be lost when attention narrows, fatigue grows, information is ambiguous or the pilot becomes overloaded. Rebuild SA by pausing, scanning flight instruments / outside, checking position and resources, and asking what has changed.

Standard radio phraseology helps sender and receiver share a common code. Communication can still fail through noise, expectation, ambiguous wording, language, readback errors or a poor headset. Listen actively, keep messages concise and read back critical clearances / instructions. If a message is unclear, ask; do not fill in missing words from expectation.

The school’s practical priority order is **fly first / keep the aircraft stable**, then navigate and communicate / diagnose. For a surprise, control the aircraft, identify the problem, prioritize, use the checklist, make a simple safe response and then verify the result. Tell ATC or a passenger what you need when useful; asking for help can lower workload.

**Beyond question bank:** CRM includes task sharing, leadership, assertiveness, monitoring, briefings and communication. In single-pilot operations, passengers may help only when the pilot gives clear, bounded instructions; they do not take responsibility away from the pilot.

## Lesson 15 — Stress and performance

Stress is a state of arousal or tension caused by environmental, physical or psychological demands. It is not always harmful: moderate arousal can increase alertness. Performance follows an inverted-U pattern in the teaching diagram. The bank labels **B** as ideal and **D** as overstrained.

Stressors can accumulate: challenging weather, time pressure, unfamiliar airports, fatigue, emotional problems, illness, noise, equipment faults or perceived lack of options. If demands exceed the pilot’s resources, the safety margin contracts. Under high stress attention narrows; the pilot may fixate, omit checklists or ignore information. Attention, concentration, responsiveness and memory are all affected.

Stress reactions differ between individuals and can change with training, confidence, sleep and experience. A problem that appears to have no solution can itself trigger stress (bank Q60). The older book discusses alarm and resistance phases and possible longer-term exhaustion; these are explanatory models, not diagnostic tools.

Useful strategies:

- **Do-strategies:** change / resolve the problem, reduce demands, divert, land or ask for help.
- **Thinking strategies:** reframe, prioritize, use a checklist, focus on the next safe action.
- **Support:** preparation, rest, fitness, good communication and discussion with trusted people.
- Symptom-only coping (e.g. excess caffeine, nicotine or alcohol) does not solve the underlying stressor and can add risk.

## Lesson 16 — Risk management and passenger care

Risk is the combination of likelihood and consequence. Before flight, identify threats from the pilot, aircraft, environment and time. Reduce, avoid or transfer exposure where possible; accept only a residual risk within personal, aircraft and legal limits. “Accept” is a conscious decision, not a synonym for neglect.

If a passenger becomes uncomfortable during cruise, the bank’s preferred response is to adjust cabin temperature and avoid excessive bank. A stable, smooth aircraft and calm communication reduce sensory conflict. Assess for illness; do not introduce unnecessary manoeuvres, speed changes or oxygen unless indicated.

## Instructor / lesson takeaways to remember

- **Preparation prevents being forced to solve a problem under pressure.** Plan weather, fuel, route, alternatives and daylight.
- **Keep margins:** personal capability, aircraft capability, environment and time all matter.
- **Trust instruments when the vestibular system and outside references disagree.** Practise instrument scan before it is needed.
- **Say what you see, verify what you hear, and challenge the first explanation.**
- **If unsure about fitness, don’t fly; if unsure about an operational limit, check the current rule / POH / instructor.**
