# Aircraft General Knowledge — Detailed Lesson Notes

These notes synthesise all five AGK slide decks (structures; engines and systems; instruments) with the 128-question bank. The decks are image-heavy, so the visual descriptions below capture labelled drawings, systems diagrams and demonstrations as well as extracted slide text. **Beyond question bank** marks useful lesson detail that was not directly required by the current bank. Always use the aircraft POH/AFM for type-specific limits and procedures.

## Deck 1 — Aircraft Structures (26 slides)

### Main components and structural members

The opening exploded-aircraft image identifies the wing, fuselage, empennage, powerplant and landing gear. The tail/empennage is made up of horizontal and vertical tail surfaces. The wing drawing labels ribs, spars, stringers, skin, wing tip, flap and aileron; it shows that the ribs establish the aerofoil profile, the spar is the major spanwise beam, and longitudinal stringers stiffen the skin. Fuselage images show frames/stringers beneath a stressed outer skin.

| Construction | Lesson explanation | Exam recognition |
|---|---|---|
| Grid / space-frame | Tubular welded framework carries loads; covering is non-self-supporting. | Q6: tubular steel construction with non-supporting skin = grid. |
| Monocoque | Outer skin/shell carries structural load. | Contrast with semi-monocoque. |
| Semi-monocoque | Frames and stringers support stressed skin and share load. | Q10–11: frames + stringers + supporting skin. |
| Sandwich / honeycomb | Thin face skins bonded around light honeycomb/foam core. | Q4–5: light core, thin layers; high stiffness/strength/stability at low mass. |

Slides show wing and fuselage cross sections; honeycomb core with constant and tapered thickness; and material strength/density comparisons. The bank’s strongest-material option is carbon-fibre reinforced plastic. The slide’s material chart is a qualitative materials map; do not infer a universal strength hierarchy for every possible alloy/composite or component.

**Beyond question bank:** weight reduction is valuable only if stiffness, fatigue life, impact resistance, repairability, corrosion and heat tolerance remain acceptable. Composite damage can be internal/delaminated and less visible than a dent; rely on inspection/maintenance data rather than visual guesswork.

### Wing configuration, loads and aircraft control

A labelled aircraft diagram maps the three axes: longitudinal (roll/ailerons), lateral (pitch/elevator) and vertical (yaw/rudder). The tail diagrams show the fin/rudder and horizontal stabilizer/elevator, stabilator and trim tabs. One image shows high-lift flaps/slats, spoilers/speedbrakes and a winglet; another points out bonding on a spoiler. The winglet reduces the strength of the tip vortex and thus induced drag.

The deck’s flight-control visuals demonstrate elevator/rudder/aileron location and how control tabs affect surfaces. For the conventional elevator picture, nose-up trim is tab down/elevator up; tab movement creates aerodynamic force that biases the main control surface. A ground-adjustable trim tab is a small non-movable tab repositioned on the ground. Balance tabs move opposite their main control surface. These are different devices and not interchangeable labels.

- Aft control input raises the nose: elevator up, tail force/downwash effect increases (bank’s wording: tail produces greater downward force).
- Left rudder gives yaw left and secondary roll left in the bank’s answer.
- Primary controls: aileron, elevator, rudder. Flaps, slats, spoilers/speedbrakes and trim are secondary.
- Load factor is lift divided by weight; gust loads and manoeuvres can overstress structure. The bank stresses gust damage when manoeuvring speed is exceeded.

**Beyond question bank:** asymmetric aileron deflection/differential ailerons and Frise ailerons can reduce adverse yaw; vortex generators energise the boundary layer; wing fences restrict spanwise flow; stall strips can encourage root-first stall. Dihedral provides lateral stability in sideslip, while anhedral reduces excessive lateral stability. These are shown or referenced in the student’s wider notes/material, but are not core answers in this bank.

### Damage, bonding and inspection

The slides show dents/creases and ask learners to inspect a real aeroplane for visible damage. Dents can result from a hard landing or excessive structural stress and must not be dismissed as paint/cosmetic issues. Bonding equalises electrical potential between conductive parts; bonding jumpers also matter during fuelling. Static discharge wicks on the aircraft dissipate charge in flight.

Gear diagrams label strut, torque arm/link, fork and axle; the bank’s numbered arrow points to the fork. The deck also illustrates a shimmy damper, wheel/tire layers and tire wear/damage. Brake diagrams show rudder pedals/toe brakes, master cylinders, a parking-brake valve and lines to the left/right main-wheel brake assemblies. On the bank item, brakes are on the main gear, not only the nose gear. Alignment marks on tire and rim show that the tire has not shifted relative to the rim.

- Nosewheel steering on a small aircraft is commonly linked to rudder pedals/rudder.
- Tire visual checks include tread and sidewall condition, pressure as specified, and alignment of tire/rim marks.
- Exceeding limitations requires the inspection prescribed for that aircraft; the bank’s keyed response is qualified-engineer inspection before next flight, despite its stray “helicopter” wording.

**Teacher tip:** on an image-ID question, start at the pointer/number and follow it to the item; don’t infer from the general aircraft photo. For configuration questions, look for a diagonal support strut and the relative height of the wing/tail.

## Deck 2 — Motoren & systemen (22 slides)

### Engine arrangements, parts and cycle

Deck begins with boxer/opposed engines used widely in small aircraft and touring motor gliders; comparison images show V, radial/star, and in-line/inverted layouts. A labelled cylinder drawing identifies camshaft, valves, spark plug, cylinder/head, piston, rings, connecting rod, crankshaft and crankcase. A four-panel sequence shows induction, compression, power and exhaust and is repeated as a circular operating-cycle diagram. A compression-ratio drawing separates swept volume from clearance volume.

| Stroke | Piston motion | Valve state / event |
|---|---|---|
| Induction | Down | Intake valve open, mixture enters. |
| Compression | Up | Both valves closed; mixture compressed. |
| Power | Down | Spark/combustion pushes piston down. |
| Exhaust | Up | Exhaust valve open; gases leave. |

The two bank figures are identifiable from piston motion and valves: AGK-007 is induction; AGK-008 is compression. One complete Otto cycle spans two crankshaft revolutions. The diagram also notes valve overlap around exhaust/intake transition.

The engine-code slide uses `LGTSIO-540-K` and associates G with geared, T turbocharged, S supercharged, I injection, O opposed, L left-turning, 540 cubic inches and K model series. Manufacturers may use different naming conventions. **Beyond question bank:** 540 in³ × 16.387 cm³/in³ ≈ 8,849 cm³ (8.85 L).

### Detonation, ignition and magneto checklist

A detonation slide describes uncontrolled spontaneous combustion/knocking and lists contributing conditions: high engine temperature, excessively lean mixture, high manifold pressure with low RPM on a constant-speed propeller, wrong/low anti-knock fuel, and abrupt throttle opening. It contrasts normal combustion with detonation after TDC. The slide lists pre-ignition/self-ignition and self-burning terminology; distinguish events carefully: pre-ignition starts before the intended spark, detonation is abnormal rapid combustion after normal ignition.

Magneto diagrams show rotating magnet, coil, breaker points, condenser, distributor, P-lead and dual magnetos/spark plugs. Magnetos are engine driven and independent of the aircraft electrical supply. Switching OFF grounds the P-lead. Engine run-up pictures show the magneto/plug test; a small RPM decrease on each magneto is expected, with return to the original RPM on BOTH. Excessive roughness suggests a defective/fouled plug. A faulty grounding circuit is hazardous because the magneto may continue firing with the key OFF.

**Teacher tip:** never generalise an RPM-drop limit from the slide; use that aircraft’s checklist. The bank’s direct facts are dual independent magnetos, expected RPM drop while OFF, recovery on BOTH, roughness likely due a bad plug, and mixture-cutoff shutdown.

### Carburettor, mixture, fuel and carburettor ice

A float-carburettor diagram labels float chamber, float, needle valve, venturi, throttle valve, mixture control, main metering jet, discharge nozzle, air bleed and accelerator pump. The carburettor’s function is to provide an ignitable air/fuel mixture; throttle controls airflow/power and mixture control adjusts fuel proportion. The primer is an auxiliary fuel-pumping device for starting.

The lesson’s icing slide says operational icing may occur below +5°C in visible moisture and lists symptoms/relevant phases: reduced power, engine run-up/idle, cruise checks, descent, slow flight, downwind/base/final, and go-around. Fuel evaporation and the venturi pressure drop cool the carburettor. Carb heat supplies warm air; with fixed-pitch propeller RPM usually drops. Use the POH; take-off is normally carb heat OFF for maximum power. The bank’s multiple-choice likelihood band is −5°C to +20°C, broader than the slide shorthand. This is not a guarantee of ice/no ice outside a temperature band.

A slide compares fuel injection with carburetion: fuel evaporates near cylinders rather than in venturi, improved distribution/metering and acceleration, but possible vapour lock and contamination. Injection avoids venturi carb ice, not all induction-system icing.

Mixture slides explain that with a fixed control setting at altitude, less air enters and the mixture becomes richer. Lesson leaning cues: cruise and appropriate lower power settings; altitude/density-altitude examples around 5,000 ft / 3,000 ft density altitude; enrich before increasing power, during descent/before landing or go-around; take-off/climb settings depend on density altitude and POH. At low altitude/full power, full rich may be specified for cooling. Too-rich symptoms: roughness, power loss, fouled plugs, high consumption, lead/soot/black smoke; it cools better and delays detonation. Too-lean/high-temperature operation: excessive temperatures, long/abnormal combustion, detonation and power loss.

**Slide caution:** the explosive-limit illustration shows rich/lean limits and numbers `1/14.7`, `1/12.5`, `6.7%`, but does not clearly specify the reference basis. Do not memorise those as a universal aircraft-mixture setting; use the conceptual rich/lean relation and POH procedure.

Fuel system drawings and notes support the bank’s facts: venting prevents tank underpressure; water settles at the lowest point; nearly empty tanks have more air space for condensation; Avgas 100LL is blue; octane is anti-knock rating. During refuelling, bond the equipment/aircraft and eliminate ignition sources, smoking and open flames. Level fuel indicators are calibrated to zero at unusable fuel in level flight per the question’s CS-23 statement.

### Cooling, oil, engine power and propeller controls

Cooling slides show airflow over cooling fins/cylinder heads, exhaust heat, oil sump and oil cooler, plus cowl flaps. Fins enlarge surface area and improve heat transfer. Poor cooling leads to high temperatures, detonation/seizure risk, oil degradation and reduced engine life. **Beyond bank:** cowl flaps regulate cooling airflow; follow the POH for climb/descent and temperature management.

Oil slides list cooling, friction reduction/lubrication, noise damping, cleaning, sealing and additives; key properties include viscosity, flash point and chemical stability. Notes encourage preflight oil-level checks, watching for leakage, and monitoring pressure/temperature after start and in flight. The slide’s maintenance shorthand mentions yearly/50-hour work and oil level checks on warm/level conditions, but intervals and exact limits are aircraft/operator-specific. Another slide says an oil-pressure indication should appear within ~30 seconds after start; use the approved checklist/limits, not this as universal clearance.

Oil-filter bypass: when filter resistance/clogging is excessive, bypass opens so oil flow continues without filtration. Do not believe a debris-catching alternate filter is necessarily present.

The lesson notes BHP, %BHP, THP, air density, manifold pressure and RPM as power-setting factors; it gives a shorthand relation BHP ≈ MAP × RPM at a given fuel flow. That is qualitative, not a calibrated equation. Constant-speed propeller procedures from the deck and bank:

- Increase power: mixture richer if required → prop RPM increase → throttle/MAP increase (bank’s two-step answer: RPM first, then MAP).
- Reduce power: throttle/MAP decrease → prop RPM decrease → mixture leaner if appropriate (bank’s two-step answer: MAP first, then RPM).

This sequencing avoids excessive manifold pressure at low RPM. Always use the engine/propeller’s specified operating envelope.

## Deck 3 — Motoren & systemen (29 slides)

Deck 3 continues engine operations and adds induction icing, injected fuel, cooling, diesel, oil and electrical-system concepts. The first slide repeats detonation causes and a diagram of normal versus abnormal combustion. The icing slide explicitly says “below +5 C in visible moisture,” with power reduction and checks at idle, cruise, descent, slow flight and approach/go-around phases. The injection slide covers metering near the cylinder, more even distribution, improved acceleration/efficiency/cooling, and vapour lock/contamination risks.

Cooling notes include exhaust heat, fins, cylinder head, oil-sump radiation, cowl flaps, climb/descent and the danger of too much/too little cooling. Listed consequences include engine seizure, detonation, high/low oil temperatures, shortened life and degraded oil. The diesel slides identify compression ignition, but the bank’s common light-aircraft/TMG engine answer is four-cylinder four-stroke Otto/opposed.

Oil-function slides expand on lubrication and heat removal with friction reduction, noise, cleaning, sealing and additives; oil quality relates to viscosity, flash point and chemical stability. The maintenance slide lists annual / 50-hour checks, preflight level, straight-and-level/warm checks, leakage, pressure after start, temperature, pressure-versus-temperature awareness, oil cooler/thermostat, cowl flaps and oil level under acceleration. These are teaching prompts; the aircraft’s maintenance programme and POH govern actual servicing/limits.

Power slide labels BHP, %BHP and THP, and says air density, MAP and RPM contribute. The “best rate of climb / best range / best endurance” slide is a topic prompt rather than a numerical performance lesson in this deck. The power-sequence slide explicitly says more power: mixture richer, prop RPM increase, throttle/MAP increase; reduce power: throttle/MAP decrease, prop RPM decrease, mixture leaner (subject to POH).

Electrical slide names battery, alternator, voltage regulator, volts, amperes, ohms/resistance, power, DC/AC, bus bars and relays/solenoids. Combine with the bank: battery and generator/alternator are sources; circuit breakers and relays are distribution/protection/control components. Alternator failure leaves the battery as temporary supply; aircraft electrical loads should be managed according to procedure.

**Beyond question bank:** identify what is engine-driven, electrically driven, vacuum-driven or pressure-driven before predicting failure effects. A light aircraft may have magnetos that continue ignition after bus failure while radio, navigation equipment and electric gyros fail as battery capacity is depleted.

## Deck 4 — Instrumenten (25 slides)

The agenda names pitot-static, compass, gyroscopic, flight-director, navigation, engine, EFIS and status indications. Much of the deck uses labelled instrument photos and pressure diagrams.

### Airspeed / pitot-static

The airspeed slides compare IAS, CAS, EAS, TAS and GS and list instrument error and pressure/position error (pitot/static ports, configuration and attitude). CAS corrects IAS for instrument/position error; EAS corrects compressibility; TAS accounts for density; GS combines TAS with wind. Bank calculation rule: add roughly 2% per 1,000 ft to CAS for TAS. The airspeed indicator compares total pitot pressure and static pressure; altimeter and VSI use static pressure.

**Teacher tip:** separate measurement from corrections: wind does not enter the pitot-static ASI equation, but it affects ground speed. Inspect pitot/static openings and surrounding airflow; contamination, blockage or a covering/film can create false indications.

### Altimeter and VSI

Slides define altimeter errors/terms: instrument error, lag, pressure/position error, dirt/blockage, leaks and moisture; altitude, height, pressure altitude, flight level, level, elevation, transition altitude and transition level. The pressure-conversion slide lists 1 hPa = 1 mbar; 1 hPa ≈ 30 ft; temperature lapse 2°C/1,000 ft and 6.5°C/1,000 m; 1013 mbar ≈ 29.92 inHg; rough ISA reference pressure levels 700 hPa/10,000 ft, 500 hPa/18,000 ft, 300 hPa/30,000 ft. The bank uses approximately 8 m per 10 hPa (= 0.8 m/hPa), i.e. about 27 ft/hPa; retain that for its numerical question.

Altimeter is a static-pressure instrument with an adjustable datum/subscale. QNH references MSL, QFE the airfield datum, standard 1013.25 hPa gives pressure altitude/flight levels. Cold air makes actual altitude lower than indicated; an altimeter cannot directly compensate temperature. Hysteresis is a mechanical error. Adjust pressure reference when required before flight and during cross-country changes.

VSI uses current static pressure and a delayed pressure in a calibrated chamber/reservoir, so it indicates rate of change with lag. Static blockage freezes the altimeter, makes VSI settle to zero, and distorts ASI. The bank’s equalising-tank question keys “too big” → indication too high.

**Teacher tip:** for a pressure-change question, first state which static/total lines are connected, then track rising/falling pressure. For “high-to-low, look out below,” the danger is the true altitude being lower than the altimeter implies.

### Direct-reading compass

Slides show the compass card, liquid damping, magnetic compass correction screws/card, compass swing, acceleration error and compass-error summary. The magnetic field has inclination/dip; it is least inclined at the magnetic equator. Variation is true-vs-magnetic north; deviation comes from aircraft magnetic fields/equipment/electrical currents. Turning and acceleration errors are additional. Gyro heading indication helps smooth out compass oscillations but must be aligned periodically.

The slide mnemonics include “Never see north / always see south,” “North lags / south leads,” and acceleration error. In the Northern Hemisphere, use UNOS: undershoot north, overshoot south. ANDS: accelerate north, decelerate south on east/west headings. Dip comes from inclination, not the aircraft’s internal magnetic field. A compass correction card is associated with a specific aircraft/compass installation.

**Beyond question bank:** compass swing/compensation is a maintenance calibration exercise, not something the pilot should adjust casually in flight. Keep portable electronics/magnetic objects away from the compass; compare the indicated magnetic heading with a DG/HI and its correction card.

## Deck 5 — Instrumenten (29 slides)

### Gyro theory and instruments

First slides show attitude, heading, turn coordinator, compass and suction gauges. The gyro principles are **rigidity in space** (rotor resists change of orientation) and **precession** (response about 90° from an applied force in the direction of rotation). A diagram shows the force plane and precession plane.

Gyro-error slides list parasitic drift (friction, bearings, imbalance, airflow), apparent drift from Earth rotation, drift due to aircraft movement, gimbal lock and tumbling. The slide quotes about 12°/hour for apparent drift away from the equator; exact apparent rate depends on latitude, and is zero at the equator and greatest near the poles. Use the slide’s value as a classroom estimate only.

A vertical-axis gyro drives the traditional artificial horizon; a horizontal-axis gyro drives the directional gyro/heading indicator. Rate gyros are used in turn instruments. Diagram sequence shows:

- Attitude indicator: miniature aeroplane/horizon shows pitch and bank.
- Directional gyro: stable heading display, needs periodic compass alignment.
- Turn coordinator versus turn-and-slip: the coordinator’s gyro is canted and responds to roll-in plus turn; both use a rate indication and an inclinometer ball for coordination.
- The ball indicates apparent vertical/slip-skid, not bank angle. A coordinated turn has the ball centred; use the appropriate rudder (“step on the ball”).

Attitude-indicator examples show level, climbs/descents and left/right banks. A two-minute turn is 3°/second; bank angle depends on TAS. The standard-rate turn’s exact bank is not a fixed value at every speed.

An integrated-display slide labels HSI/PFD: HSI combines directional-gyro heading with VHF navigation receiver/course information. ADI combines attitude indicator with flight-director commands. Electronic flight displays can combine more sensor sources; distinguish the terms asked in the bank.

### Suction, pressure and temperature instruments

Vacuum-system diagram labels vacuum pump, regulator/relief valve, filter, suction gauge and vacuum-driven heading/attitude gyros. Failure of suction may affect those gyros even if the electrical bus is healthy. Electrical instruments instead depend on the electrical system. The bank’s pressure-instrument list includes oil/fuel/MAP pressures, altimeter, VSI, ASI and suction indication.

Oil-pressure slides show pressure gauges/transducers; oil-temperature and engine-temperature slides show oil temp, CHT, EGT and turbine examples ITT/TIT. CHT probes often monitor the most critical cylinder, not every cylinder. The deck’s temperature-gauge question includes oil, coolant, CHT, EGT, outside-air and cabin temperature.

### Status lights / annunciators

Slides show engine-monitor digital displays, fuel quantity/flow, warning panels and caution annunciators. Colour convention in the bank: red identifies operating limits/warning; amber/orange means corrective action may be needed in the future; green generally denotes normal. Exact meanings are aircraft-specific—read the legend/AFM.

**Teacher tip:** instrument questions ask either (1) what quantity is measured, (2) which source drives it, (3) how a fault changes the indication, or (4) what a colour/arc means. Answer that one layer only. Do not infer “electric” merely because a modern glass cockpit displays an instrument; the sensor architecture may be different.

## Bank coverage and discrepancies

All 128 questions have been cross-checked against the cheat sheet. No bank question remained uncovered. Points where source wording deserves care:

- **Q91:** question text says “helicopter” although it sits in the aeroplane AGK bank; keyed answer: qualified engineer inspection before next flight after limitations exceeded. Treat as exam key, not universal maintenance instruction.
- **Q109:** keyed carburettor-icing band is −5°C to +20°C; lesson slide says below +5°C in visible moisture. These are not truly exclusive: icing risk depends on temperature, humidity, power and carburettor cooling; learn the bank option and use POH procedures.
- **Q62:** oversized VSI equalising tank → indication too high is the bank answer. Tank/instrument designs can differ; this is a keyed instrument-design question.
- The slide’s approximate gyro drift rate (~12°/h) is not a universal constant; Earth-rate effect varies with latitude.
- The mixture-slide ratio/percentage labels are ambiguous without a stated reference; do not adopt them as an aircraft setting.
- The deck’s maintenance notes (annual/50 h, warm/level oil check, +4/−2 shorthand) are aircraft-specific shorthand, not universally applicable inspection intervals/limits.
